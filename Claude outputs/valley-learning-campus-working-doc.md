# Valley of Grace Learning Campus: Working Document

**Status:** Brainstorm / feasibility exploration
**Date:** 16 September 2026
**Author:** Michael Haupt, with AI assistance (Claude, Anthropic)

---

## The problem this solves

The valley has no English-medium high school. The nearest is in Caledon, 35 km away. The Decision Tool maps the consequences: fragmented peer groups, transport costs, families leaving.

A standalone English-medium high school is not economically viable. The catchment area produces too few learners to sustain conventional staffing, infrastructure, and fee levels. Every model in the Decision Tool's immediate options involves trade-offs that most families find unacceptable in at least one dimension.

At the same time, the Ward 2 Business and Community Development Forum (inaugural meeting, 16 September 2026) identified five development priorities, four of which have an education or training component that the valley currently cannot deliver locally:

1. Tourism: no trained guides, no accredited training, no tourism office.
2. Skills/education: skills gap in agriculture (mechanical, technical, computer), Elsenburg occupational qualifications unknown and underused, no local skills centre.
3. Agriculture/food sovereignty: community gardens fallow, water rights unused, small-scale farmer development needed.
4. Conservation: invasive species clearing requires trained teams, ecological restoration is a prerequisite for tourism.
5. Business development: small businesses need basic management, financial literacy, digital skills.

The hypothesis: a single integrated facility serving three functions (high school, bioregional learning centre, skills centre) creates economic viability that none of the three can achieve alone. Shared infrastructure, shared staff, multiple revenue streams, and a community-wide constituency replace the narrow base that kills a standalone school.

## The three components

### 1. English-medium high school

**What it is:** A registered independent school offering CAPS (or equivalent) from Grade 8 to 12, English medium, with a bioregional curriculum orientation. Small: 60 to 120 learners across five year groups at maturity, starting smaller.

**Regulatory path:** Register with the WCED under SASA Section 46. Choose an examination body: SACAI (lower administrative burden, used by many smaller independents and online providers) or IEB (higher prestige, higher cost, better fit if the school aims at a university-track reputation). Meet CAPS equivalence requirements. The Decision Tool's Variable 3 covers this in detail.

**What makes it different from Silverwood or a generic independent school:** The curriculum is bioregionally grounded. Geography uses the Riviersonderend watershed. History uses Genadendal's 300-year mission history. Life Sciences uses fynbos ecology. Tourism, Agricultural Sciences, and Business Studies draw on the valley's actual economy. This is not a pedagogical aspiration bolted onto a standard school; it is the school's organising principle, and it is shared with the other two components.

**Subject availability (FET):** The small-school problem is real (Variable 7). A school of 60 to 120 cannot offer the full CAPS FET menu. But if the skills centre component runs occupational qualifications in tourism, agriculture, and conservation, some of those programmes can serve as enrichment or elective pathways for Grade 10 to 12 learners. A learner studying Agricultural Sciences at school can do practical components through the Elsenburg-linked programme running on the same campus. A learner taking Tourism can train towards a CATHSSETA-accredited guide qualification alongside adult learners. The integration is the answer to the small-school subject menu problem.

**Relationship to Emil Weder:** This is not a competitor. Emil Weder serves the Afrikaans-medium community and will continue to. The English-medium high school serves families for whom Afrikaans instruction is not workable. Some families may choose Emil Weder for the diversity and the Afrikaans immersion; others need English. Both can coexist. The skills centre and bioregional learning centre components serve everyone in the ward, including Emil Weder learners who want supplementary programmes.


### 2. Bioregional Learning Centre (BLC)

**What it is:** A place-based education and research facility grounded in the valley's ecology, heritage, and economy. It is not a school and not a training centre; it is the connective tissue that gives the other two components their distinctive character and that serves the wider community.

**Functions:**

- **Curriculum development and delivery** for the high school. The BLC produces the bioregional modules that make the school's CAPS delivery distinctive: watershed studies, fynbos ecology field programmes, heritage interpretation of Genadendal's mission history, citizen science protocols (leopard monitoring, bioprecipitation research, BioScore).

- **Community education programmes.** Open to all valley residents. Heritage walks, ecological literacy, food sovereignty workshops, indigenous knowledge documentation. These are not accredited programmes; they are community capacity building. They generate foot traffic, community buy-in, and a constituency that sustains the broader institution.

- **Research and documentation hub.** The 2038 tricentenary strategy, the TRANCRAA documentation, the leopard corridor mapping, the bioprecipitation research, the BioConomy wiki: all of this work needs a physical home and an institutional anchor. The BLC is that anchor. It hosts visiting researchers, international bioregional visitors, and community working groups.

- **Tourism product.** The BLC is itself a tourism destination. Heritage and ecological tours departing from the centre, led by CATHSSETA-accredited guides trained on site, generate revenue and demonstrate the valley's proposition. This directly addresses the W2BF's first priority.

**What it is not:** It is not a museum (that is David's domain at the Genadendal Mission Museum). It is not a conservation NGO (that is CapeNature's role). It operates alongside both and creates programming that draws on both.


### 3. Community Skills Centre

**What it is:** An accredited training facility offering occupational qualifications, short courses, and learnerships aligned with the valley's economic needs. It serves adults (working-age community members, emerging farmers, tourism entrants, school leavers) and, where appropriate, high school learners in integrated programmes.

**Regulatory path (not a TVET college):** The centre does not register as a TVET college (that requires DHET establishment, which is a national-level process and unrealistic for a ward-level initiative). Instead, it operates as a QCTO-accredited Skills Development Provider (SDP). SDP accreditation is valid for five years and allows the centre to offer occupational qualifications and occupational skills programmes under QCTO quality assurance. Separately, it seeks programme-specific accreditation from relevant SETAs.

**Potential partnership: CET satellite.** The Western Cape CET College operates an Overberg Community Learning Centre with three satellite centres. Riviersonderend already has one (125 Disa Ave, Riviersonderend). Genadendal does not. If the campus applies to host a CET satellite learning centre, it gains state-funded adult basic education (AET Levels 1-4 and GETC, equivalent to Grade 9). This serves community members who did not complete schooling and creates a pipeline into the occupational qualifications. The CET satellite is a low-cost addition (state-funded educators, state curriculum) that dramatically broadens the constituency.

## The viability thesis

A standalone high school of 60 learners paying R30,000/year generates R1.8 million. That covers maybe three teachers. It cannot sustain a facility.

The combined model changes the arithmetic:

**Revenue streams:**
- School fees (60-120 learners). Even at modest independent school rates.
- SETA-funded learnerships and skills programmes. SETAs pay per learner per programme. CATHSSETA, AgriSETA, and Services SETA all fund programmes aligned with the valley's needs. The centre claims funding for each enrolled adult learner.
- CET satellite allocation. State-funded: the DHET pays for educators and materials at the satellite learning centre.
- Short course fees. Unaccredited community programmes (heritage walks, ecological literacy, food sovereignty) can charge modest fees or be sponsored.
- Tourism revenue. Heritage and ecological tours departing from the campus, run as a social enterprise.
- Grant funding. The TGF application (R1.4 million, already in process) covers heritage trail infrastructure and guide training. The bioregional learning centre is a natural recipient for conservation, heritage, and education grants.
- Donor and CSI funding. An integrated community learning campus aligned with national skills priorities, conservation, and heritage is a compelling funding proposition.

**Cost sharing:**
- Classrooms are used for school during the day and adult programmes in the evenings and on Saturdays.
- A computer lab serves school IT, adult digital skills, and CET satellite learners.
- A practical workshop (kitchen, basic tools) serves hospitality training, agricultural processing, and school practical subjects.
- A meeting/conference space serves community forums, W2BF meetings, tourism briefings, and school assemblies.
- Teachers with dual qualifications deliver school subjects and adult programmes. A teacher qualified in Tourism and holding CATHSSETA facilitator accreditation teaches Tourism to Grade 10-12 learners during the day and runs adult guide training in intensive blocks.

**Constituency:**
- A standalone English-medium school serves 20 to 30 Greyton families. Narrow base, vulnerable to fluctuation.
- The combined campus serves the entire ward: Genadendal adults needing literacy (CET), emerging farmers needing qualifications (AgriSETA), aspiring tour guides (CATHSSETA), school leavers entering the local economy, high school learners, commercial farmers sending staff for training, and tourists arriving for heritage tours. The constituency is the ward, not a suburb.

## Institutional architecture

### Legal entity

A Non-Profit Company (NPC) registered under the Companies Act. The NPC holds all three functions. It registers separately as:
- An independent school (WCED, SASA Section 46)
- A Skills Development Provider (QCTO)
- A CET satellite host (agreement with Western Cape CET College)

The NPC structure allows multiple registrations, accepts grant and donor funding, qualifies for Section 18A tax-deductible donation status (if registered as a Public Benefit Organisation with SARS), and can operate a social enterprise (tourism) within its objects.

### Governance

The NPC board must reflect the ward, not one community. This is the protocol issue George Juries raised at the W2BF meeting: do not develop proposals and invite people to endorse them. The board composition is a design question from day one.

Possible board structure:
- Genadendal community representatives (Transformasie Komitee nominee, at least one other)
- Greyton community representative(s)
- Commercial farming sector representative
- TWKM economic development (ex officio, advisory)
- CapeNature or conservation sector representative
- Parent representative (once the school is operational)
- Independent education or skills development specialist

The Transformasie Komitee's 8-sectorial plan should inform the campus programme design. The Komitee is a stakeholder in governance, not a subordinate partner.

### Naming

The W2BF agreed on "Ward 2 Business and Community Development Forum." The learning campus needs a name that signals integration, not one community's ownership. Working name for this document: **Valley of Grace Learning Campus**. The name should be settled through a community process, not imposed.

## Phasing

The combined model does not need to launch all three components simultaneously. A phased approach reduces risk and builds capacity.

### Phase 0: Groundwork (now to mid-2027)
- Convene a working group through the W2BF (November 2026 meeting).
- Engage the Transformasie Komitee formally, following the protocol Keith Radmore committed to.
- Commission or assemble a feasibility study covering demand (how many adults for which programmes, how many high school learners, how many tourism visitors).
- Approach the Western Cape CET College about hosting a satellite learning centre in Genadendal.
- Approach Elsenburg about satellite delivery of agricultural occupational qualifications, with valley commercial farmers as host farms.
- Apply for CATHSSETA accreditation as a training provider for tourist guide qualifications.
- Identify a site. (See open questions below.)

### Phase 1: Skills centre and CET satellite (2027-2028)
- Launch the CET satellite (adult basic education, AET Levels 1-4).
- Launch the first SETA-funded skills programmes: tourist guide training (CATHSSETA), agricultural occupational qualifications via Elsenburg partnership, basic business skills.
- Begin community education programmes through the BLC: heritage walks, ecological literacy, food sovereignty.
- Generate revenue, build a track record, demonstrate demand.

### Phase 2: High school (2028-2029)
- Register the independent school with the WCED (apply 12-18 months before intended opening).
- Open with Grade 8 only, growing one year group at a time.
- First cohort writes matric in 2032 or 2033 depending on start year.
- High school learners participate in skills centre programmes from day one (Tourism elective learners join guide training; Agricultural Sciences learners join Elsenburg practical blocks).

### Phase 3: Maturation (2029-2033)
- Full five-year-group high school operational.
- Skills centre running multiple SETA-funded programmes annually.
- BLC hosting research, international visitors, and regular tourism programming.
- Campus is a recognisable institution in the Overberg, not a collection of pilot projects.

## The Elsenburg connection (specific)

The W2BF meeting surfaced this clearly. The Dept of Agriculture representative described the occupational qualifications programme in detail: NQF Level 3 (Grade 9/10 entry or farm experience) and Level 4 (matric or equivalent). Fully funded by the Department. Semester structure: a month at Elsenburg, a month on a host farm. Stipend of R60/day during college weeks. Applications open 1 April to 30 June annually.

The bottleneck is the host farm requirement. Applicants from Genadendal don't know how to navigate the process and don't have connections to host farms.

The campus model solves this directly:
- The skills centre becomes the local coordination point for Elsenburg applications.
- Commercial farmers in the valley (Ivan Steenkamp at The Oaks, Guy, others) register as host farms.
- The skills centre handles the matching, the paperwork, and the pastoral support.
- Elsenburg already runs decentralised short courses at Bredasdorp (60 km from Genadendal). Running practical blocks closer to home is a small step.

This does not require building anything. It requires coordination, which is what the campus institution provides.

---

## The CATHSSETA connection (specific)

Paul Adendorf holds both cultural and adventure tour guide qualifications. The valley's tourism strategy (TGF application, leopard corridor, heritage trails) requires trained, accredited guides. There are only 720 registered guides in the Western Cape. The demand exists.

The Western Cape Government has a formal application process to become an accredited provider of tourism training and assessment. CATHSSETA also accredits training providers directly and funds learnership programmes.

The campus model:
- Applies for CATHSSETA training provider accreditation.
- Paul Adendorf (and others with qualifications) serve as facilitators.
- The BLC provides the content (heritage interpretation, fynbos ecology, leopard corridor).
- Adult learners complete the tourist guide qualification and enter the valley's tourism economy.
- High school learners taking Tourism as a CAPS subject do their practical components through the same programme.
- The TGF grant (if awarded) funds the first cohort's training infrastructure.

## Open questions (hard ones first)

### 1. Site

Where does this go? Three possibilities, each with political and practical implications:

- **Genadendal.** Closest to the largest population. Most aligned with the community development purpose. The Hester Dorothea Conference Centre or nearby buildings could serve as an initial venue. But: the TRANCRAA process on Farm 39 complicates any building or land use. And a Genadendal-located campus may not attract Greyton families who want an English-medium school for their children.

- **Greyton.** Closer to the English-speaking families whose children are the high school's primary constituency. But: locating the campus in Greyton risks repeating the protocol failure George Juries identified. A Greyton-based institution serving the ward must earn its legitimacy with the Genadendal community, which is harder if it's not physically there.

- **Between the two, or on Farm 39 land.** Symbolically powerful. Practically complicated (access, services, TRANCRAA).

This is a governance question as much as a logistics question. The site decision signals whose institution this is.

### 2. Relationship to Silverwood

The Decision Tool profiles Silverwood as a proposed independent school. If Silverwood proceeds, does the campus model compete with it, absorb it, or operate alongside it? The campus model is broader than Silverwood (which appears to be school-only), but there may be overlap in intent and constituency. This needs a direct conversation with the Silverwood proponents.

### 3. Emil Weder relationship

Can high school learners at Emil Weder access the skills centre and BLC programmes? If yes, the campus serves as a supplementary resource for the existing school, not a rival. Emil Weder learners doing supplementary tourism or agricultural training through the campus creates integration rather than competition. This requires a relationship with Emil Weder's SGB and staff.

### 4. Staffing model

The combined model assumes staff who can work across components. A Tourism teacher who also facilitates CATHSSETA adult training. An Agriculture teacher who coordinates the Elsenburg programme. A conservation specialist who runs BLC field programmes and teaches Life Sciences. These are not standard teacher profiles. Recruitment and development matter.

### 5. Minimum viable enrolment

What is the smallest configuration that works? If the high school opens with 15 Grade 8 learners and the skills centre serves 40 adults per year across all programmes, is that enough to sustain the infrastructure? The revenue model needs numbers. The feasibility study in Phase 0 must answer this.

### 6. The protocol question

George Juries was clear: do not bring proposals and ask for endorsement. The campus concept must be developed with the Genadendal community from the start. That means the working group convened through the W2BF must include Genadendal voices before any proposal is written for external consumption. This working document is a thinking tool for Michael; the community-facing version must be co-authored.

### 7. Water and land rights

Farm 39's communal registration includes domestic and agricultural water rights. Paul Adendorf raised this at the meeting. If the campus model includes agricultural training on community land using community water, the TRANCRAA process and the Communal Property Association become central stakeholders. The campus cannot plan around Farm 39 resources without engaging the CPA and the TRANCRAA process directly.

### 8. Accreditation timeline

QCTO SDP accreditation, CATHSSETA training provider accreditation, WCED school registration, and CET satellite establishment each have their own timelines. Some can run in parallel. The feasibility study should map these timelines against the phasing plan. A realistic launch for the skills centre component is 12 to 18 months from application; the school component is 18 to 24 months.

---

## What this document does not do

- It does not propose a budget. That requires the feasibility study.
- It does not commit to a site.
- It does not commit to a governance structure.
- It does not speak for the Genadendal community or the Transformasie Komitee.
- It does not replace the Decision Tool, which remains the resource for families making immediate high school decisions for 2028.

What it does is test whether the combination of three functions (high school, bioregional learning centre, skills centre) creates something viable that none of the three could be alone, and whether that combination addresses the needs the W2BF identified. The answer, on paper, is yes. Whether it works in practice depends on whether the community wants it and whether the governance can hold.
