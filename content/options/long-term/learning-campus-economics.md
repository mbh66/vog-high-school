How the Learning Campus teaches economics across both coordination layers: the conventional market economy and the [BioConomy](https://wiki.bioconomy.earth).

**This page describes a proposed curriculum component. The Learning Campus itself is not yet constituted. See [[learning-campus|Learning Campus]] for the full option profile and [[learning-campus-actions|Actions Required]] for the implementation pathway.**

## The two economies

The Learning Campus operates within two economies simultaneously, and its students learn both.

The first is the market economy: goods and services priced in rand, exchanged through money, governed by supply and demand. This is the +M layer in Ronfeldt's [TIMN framework](https://wiki.bioconomy.earth/frameworks/timn-framework), the coordination form that shapes formal employment, taxation, trade, and the national curriculum's treatment of economics and business. CAPS Economics and Business Studies teach this layer thoroughly. Students who complete matric need to understand it because the labour market, higher education, and the tax system all run on it.

The second is the BioConomy: coordination through networks of mutual commitment, drawing on reciprocity traditions older than money and revived through contemporary practice. This is the +E (Emergent) layer in the [TIME framework](https://wiki.bioconomy.earth/frameworks/time-framework). It operates alongside the market economy. It does not replace it. It makes visible and mobilizable the capacities that markets cannot price: skills exchanged between neighbours, labour contributed to shared projects, ecological knowledge passed between generations, and land access negotiated through relationship.

The valley holds both economies in daily life. A farmer sells produce at the Saturday market (+M) and contributes labour to a neighbour's harvest through a rotating work arrangement (+E). A heritage guide earns rand from tourists (+M) and deepens the community's capacity to interpret its own history (+E). The campus curriculum teaches students to see both layers operating, to work competently within each, and to understand why one without the other leaves a community structurally incomplete.

## CAPS alignment

CAPS Economics (Grade 10-12) covers microeconomics, macroeconomics, economic pursuits, and contemporary economic issues. CAPS Business Studies covers business environments, business ventures, business roles, and business operations. The BioConomy content does not displace any of this. It supplements it.

The supplementary content enters through two channels already available within CAPS:

**Contemporary economic issues.** CAPS Economics includes a module on contemporary economic issues including inflation, economic growth, and redress. The BioConomy content extends this to cover commons-based coordination, community currencies, and the distinction between cash poverty and resource poverty. These are documented, operating economic practices (Grassroots Economics Foundation, Kenya; community exchange networks across Southern Africa), not speculative theory.

**Practical application through the skills centre.** The Learning Campus integrates high school subjects with practical skills centre programmes. A student studying Business Studies who also participates in a campus-based commitment pool is learning business operations in both economies at once. The pool itself, with its four protocol functions (curation, valuation, limitation, exchange), is a live business operation that the student helps govern.

## What the students learn

### The TIME framework

Four coordination forms that human societies have developed, each building on the ones before:

**Tribes (T).** Kinship and mutual obligation. Resources coordinated through relationship and reciprocity. The oldest form. Present in the valley through Khoi heritage, through ubuntu, letsema, and stokvels, and through the daily reciprocity of neighbours who know each other.

**Institutions (I).** Formalized trust. Rules that hold between strangers. The Moravian mission at Genadendal, founded in 1738, is an Institution in this sense: it governed land, labour, and trade through church authority for over 250 years. Government, schools, and the legal system are Institutions.

**Markets (M).** Competitive exchange through money and prices. Connects strangers across distance. Powerful and efficient. Blind to anything it cannot price. This is the layer the CAPS curriculum teaches.

**Emergent (E).** Networks of mutual commitment operating at scale. Commitment pooling, community exchange systems, commons-based governance. This draws on Tribal reciprocity but uses formal protocols (the four functions below) to operate beyond the limits of personal memory and reputation.

The valley is the only documented place where all four layers are visible in a single geography, each with its own institutional memory. Students who grow up here can study the framework as local history and present reality.

### Commitment pooling and the four protocol functions

From Will Ruddick's work with the Grassroots Economics Foundation (documented in *[Grassroots Economics: Reflection and Practice](https://wiki.bioconomy.earth/sources/ruddick-grassroots-economics-2025)*, 2025), students learn the operational mechanics of BioConomy coordination:

**Curation.** What belongs in the pool? Which commitments are trustworthy and useful enough to include? This is a governance question: the community (or the pool's members) decides what qualifies. Not everything does.

**Valuation.** What is each commitment worth relative to others? If one hour of tutoring and two hours of garden labour are treated as equivalent, that is a valuation decision. Valuation in a commitment pool is explicit and negotiated, unlike market pricing, which emerges from aggregate supply and demand.

**Limitation.** How much can any member contribute or withdraw before the balance shifts? Without limitation, a pool can be flooded with promises that cannot be kept, or drained by members who take without returning. This is the discipline function.

**Exchange.** How do commitments circulate? Directly between members, through the pool, or through chains of exchange where a baker's bread vouchers eventually reach a carpenter's labour vouchers through intermediate steps?

These four functions map onto governance, accounting, risk management, and operations. Students who understand them in the context of a commitment pool also understand them in a conventional business. The skills transfer in both directions.

### The colonial economics of "poverty"

CAPS includes redress and economic history. The campus curriculum extends this to cover a specific distortion: the word "poverty" as applied to communities that are self-sufficient but cash-poor.

Colonization did not arrive in a land without economies. It arrived in a land with economies that did not use money as their primary coordination mechanism. The Mweria tradition among the Mijikenda of coastal Kenya, where three of four working days were spent in mutual service through rotating labour, was an economic system with its own logic and its own efficiencies. Describing such communities as "poor" because they did not participate in cash economies was a political act, not an economic observation.

Students in the valley encounter this distortion in their own lives. Families rich in skills, land, relationships, heritage, and ecological knowledge register as economically marginal because those capacities do not pass through the price mechanism. The curriculum names this clearly: cash-poor is not resource-poor. The distinction is not sentimental. It is analytical, and it has practical consequences for how the valley develops.

## What the students implement

The economics module is not purely theoretical. The Learning Campus, by design, operates within both economies, and students participate in both.

### Market economy participation

Students engage with the +M economy through conventional channels already embedded in CAPS and the skills centre:

Business Studies practical projects (business plans, market research, financial statements). Skills centre programmes that lead to formal qualifications and employment (CATHSSETA tourist guiding, Elsenburg agricultural qualifications). School-based enterprises: the BLC's heritage and ecological tours generate tourism revenue. Financial literacy: budgeting, taxation, banking, investment.

These are standard. The campus does them with the advantage of a live economic environment: the valley's tourism, agriculture, and conservation sectors provide real clients, real revenue, and real consequences.

### BioConomy participation

Students engage with the +E economy through structured programmes that make the second coordination layer operational:

**Campus commitment pool.** The campus runs a commitment pool among its community: students, staff, parents, skills centre participants, and BLC volunteers. Members contribute commitments (hours of labour, specific skills, materials, mentorship) and draw on commitments from others. The pool is governed through the four protocol functions. Students participate in its governance as members of the pool with real commitments and real accountability.

Practically, this means a student who cannot pay full school fees in rand may be able to contribute through the pool: labour on the campus garden, mentorship of younger students, assistance with BLC programming, skilled work learned through the skills centre. This is not charity and not a discount. It is a second coordination mechanism with its own accounting. The Transformasie Komitee and the W2BF working group are the governance bodies through which the pool's curation and limitation functions would operate.

**Community exchange documentation.** Students in the BLC research component document existing reciprocity practices in the valley: rotating labour arrangements among farmers, informal childcare networks, material-sharing among neighbours, the stokvel system. This documentation serves two purposes. It provides primary-source material for the campus's economics curriculum. And it makes the +E layer visible to institutions (including funders and government) that tend to see only the +M layer.

**Ecological commitment accounting.** Conservation and alien vegetation clearing are W2BF development priorities. Students trained in ecological monitoring (through CapeNature partnership and the BLC) contribute ecological data and restoration labour. These contributions have measurable value: hectares cleared, water yield improved, biodiversity surveyed. The campus tracks this value in both registers: the rand cost of equivalent commercial clearing (for grant reporting and SETA compliance) and the commitment-pool contribution (for internal governance).

## Foundation: the Class 6 curriculum

A parallel curriculum has been designed for Silverwood School's Class 6 Business Mathematics block: ten 90-minute modules introducing the TIME framework, commitment pooling, and the four protocol functions through story, games, and applied arithmetic. The modules use narratives from Ruddick's work (the Magic Calabash, Mweria, the Pumpkin parable, Magic Coin Soup) and culminate in students designing their own commitment pools for real needs in the school or community.

Children who complete this introduction at primary level arrive at the Learning Campus with the conceptual vocabulary already in place. The high school curriculum deepens and formalizes what the Class 6 modules introduce through narrative. Students who enter the campus without this foundation receive the core concepts through the CAPS-aligned supplementary content described above.
