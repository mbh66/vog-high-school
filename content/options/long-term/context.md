# Treverton College and Environmental Education Pioneering: A Research Brief

## Lessons for the Valley of Grace Learning Campus


---

## 1. Treverton: Origins and Campus

Treverton Preparatory School and College sits on a 270-hectare estate in Mooi River, in the KwaZulu-Natal Midlands. The original institution was established by Peter Binns in the 1940s, closed in the late 1950s, and was revived in 1964 when Rev. Sydney Hudson-Reed and a group of Baptist leaders purchased the derelict property and restored it through volunteer work parties. The reopened Preparatory School started with 51 pupils under headmaster Derek Hudson-Reed. By 1970 it had produced its first Matric class.

The campus expanded when the Mooi River Municipality donated 73 acres of adjoining land and the Treverton Trust acquired neighbouring farmland. Girls were admitted from 1978 (Prep) and 1979 (College). In 1982 the school began enrolling students regardless of race, the same year it hosted the conference that launched EEASA. The school currently enrols approximately 400 students across Prep (150) and College (250) and operates as an inter-denominational Christian institution using the Independent Examinations Board.

Two institutional histories have been published: *Hope Beyond* (1985) and *Hope Beyond Expectations* (2004).

The 270-hectare estate encompasses multiple ecosystems: wetlands, grasslands, indigenous forests, and dams. It is large enough to contain a dedicated wildlife area, equestrian centre, trail-running and mountain-biking routes, and extensive sports fields, while still functioning as a working educational campus.

## 2. The 1982 EEASA Conference: What Happened

On 3 April 1982 Treverton College hosted a national environmental education gathering. During that conference a small group decided to establish a formal association and formed a working group to draft a constitution. This was the founding moment of the Environmental Education Association of Southern Africa (EEASA).

From the start, EEASA adopted what it called "a holistic way" of viewing environmental education. The founders insisted that environmental education could not be confined to nature study: it had to encompass social, political, and economic dimensions. This was 1982, in apartheid South Africa, which made the position quietly radical. Environmental education became a bridge discipline, connecting ecology with justice, land, community, and governance.

EEASA's past presidents include Dr Presha Ramsarup, Mumsie Gumede, Mphemelang Ketlhoilwe, Justin Lupele, Innocent Hodzonge, Sibongile Masuku van Damme, Eureta Janse van Rensburg, Rams Ramutlha, Jim Taylor, and Frances Gamble. The association regionalised across southern Africa during the 1990s and has maintained partnerships with Rhodes University (the Environmental Learning Research Centre), the University of Stellenbosch, UNISA, and institutions across the subcontinent.

Pat Irwin compiled a 25-year institutional history: *EEASA in a Changing World: An Historical Review at 25 Years* (2007).

The choice of Treverton as host was not accidental. A boarding school on 270 hectares of Midlands grassland, wetland, and forest, integrating racial desegregation in the same year, offered a physical container large enough and a philosophical posture open enough to hold a conversation about environment-as-whole-system. The campus was the argument made visible.

## 3. What Treverton Built After 1982

The 1982 conference was a beginning, not a peak. Treverton went on to develop three layers of environmental programming, each building on the last.

### 3.1 The Treverton Wildlife Area (established 1999/2000)

In 1999 Treverton negotiated with the local Agricultural Association and provincial conservation authorities to convert 15 hectares of former cattle farmland into a conservation site with an adjoining 15-hectare wetland. This became the Treverton Wildlife Area, described as the first natural wildlife and game area in South Africa entirely managed by school pupils and staff.

The area contains seven distinct ecosystems, with herds of zebra and blesbok. Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife designated it a "Site of Conservation Significance" in 2005.

Student management takes two forms:

- Teacher-driven action learning projects for lower grades
- Learner-driven projects for senior grades
- Grade 7 learners conduct annual wetland rehabilitation as part of their Environmental Education curriculum

Active conservation work includes wetland rehabilitation (drain blocking), exotic plant control, erosion management, grassland management, and species reintroduction.

The Wildlife and Environment Society of South Africa (WESSA) awarded Treverton its Corporate Award in 2012 for these efforts. The school holds Eco-School status.

### 3.2 Outdoor Education Program (Grades 1-12)

Treverton's outdoor education programme is compulsory and progressive. It begins in Grade 1 and escalates through the school:

- Compulsory Sunday outdoor activities for boarders
- Weekend hikes and mountain adventures in the Drakensberg
- Overnight camps from Grade 4, increasing in duration through the senior phase
- Conservation-focused programming concentrated in Grade 7

The programme is explicitly non-competitive and non-punitive: "competition is limited and punitive assessments and tests are not the measure of a person's value." The approach is grounded in "education of our learners for and towards sustainable living and growth."

### 3.3 Contemporary Educational Innovation

Treverton's recent curriculum innovations show how environmental and place-based education now permeate the whole school, not only the outdoor programme:

**Wacky Wednesdays.** Students apply classroom theory to real-world problems without prior preparation. One session had Grade 9 learners deploying Arduino water sensors at the school dam, managing a fictional currency, and making purchasing decisions, integrating science, technology, and accounting in a single exercise.

**Unplugged Days.** Practical life skills absent from the standard curriculum: tax filing, cooking, carpentry, sewing, plumbing, survival skills (Grades 8-12).

**Stewardship Programme.** Integrated into the Outdoor Pursuits curriculum for Grades 8-10. Students study permaculture theory before building raised-bed vegetable gardens, with planned expansion into recycling, solar energy, and water conservation.

Dean Balie, who oversees Grade 8-9 academics, frames the school's direction around the fact that "65% of grade 8-9 students will work in jobs that don't exist yet." The school's stated mission is to teach students "to think and how to be effective problem-solvers."

## 4. The Treverton Model: Structural Features Worth Isolating

Before drawing lessons for the Valley of Grace, it helps to name the structural features that make Treverton's environmental work durable, as distinct from the specific programmes.

1. **Scale of land.** 270 hectares is large enough to contain multiple ecosystems, a wildlife area, an equestrian centre, a working farm component, trail networks, and all the conventional school infrastructure. The campus is itself a landscape, not a building set inside one.

2. **Student governance of conservation.** The Wildlife Area is managed by pupils and staff, not outsourced to a conservation contractor. The pedagogical loop is closed: students learn ecology by doing ecology, and the conservation area's health depends on what they learn.

3. **Institutional memory.** The 1982 conference created an associational anchor (EEASA) that outlived any individual champion. The school's environmental identity is now encoded in its WESSA status, its Eco-School certification, its published histories, and a 40-year track record. No single retirement or budget cycle can erase it.

4. **Progressive curriculum integration.** Environmental education is not a standalone subject. It is threaded through outdoor education (compulsory from Grade 1), through Wacky Wednesdays and Unplugged Days (Grades 8-12), through the Stewardship Programme (permaculture, Grades 8-10), and through the Wildlife Area management (Grade 7 and above). Each programme feeds the next.

5. **Trust-based governance.** The Treverton Trust owns the land and allocates funds to the environmental programme. This creates institutional continuity independent of government education budgets or municipal land-use decisions.

6. **A holistic definition of "environment."** From the 1982 EEASA founding onward, Treverton's environmental education has included social, political, and economic dimensions. This is not nature study. It is systems literacy taught through a landscape.

7. **External recognition as a ratchet.** Eco-School status, the WESSA Corporate Award, the Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife "Site of Conservation Significance" designation: each external recognition raises the reputational cost of retreating from environmental commitments. Recognition is a governance mechanism, not decoration.

## 5. Lessons for the Valley of Grace Learning Campus

The Ward 2 Schools Program already proposes curriculum-embedded environmental education, citizen-science integration through the GoE Realm, an inter-school contest, and a Bioregional Learning Centre. The Treverton precedent offers seven specific lessons for sharpening that design.

### 5.1 The campus must be a landscape, not a site

Treverton's 270 hectares are not incidental. The campus-as-landscape is what makes "education for biodiversity" credible: students can walk from a classroom to a wetland, from a dam to a grassland, from an equestrian yard to a wildlife area. The learning environment and the subject matter are the same place.

**VoG application.** The Valley of Grace Realm covers approximately 10,000 hectares. The proposed Bioregional Learning Centre should be designed as a node within that landscape, with defined learning corridors extending into the Greyton commonage (2,200 hectares), the Greyton Nature Reserve, and the Farm 39 TRANCRAA land. The centre is the base; the valley is the campus.

### 5.2 Students must govern, not only observe

Treverton's Wildlife Area works because students manage it. They make decisions about wetland rehabilitation, alien clearing, erosion control, and species reintroduction. The conservation area's condition is their responsibility and their record.

**VoG application.** The GoE Realm structure already positions learners as data producers (BioScore contributions through iNaturalist). The next step is governance: giving schools adopted sections of the valley's ecology (the inter-school contest already proposes restoration naming rights). Treverton's experience says this works, but it requires a defined parcel of land, a management brief, and a supervision structure. The Bioregional Learning Centre could allocate specific restoration blocks to participating schools for ongoing stewardship.

### 5.3 Start with a conference, not a curriculum

EEASA was born from a gathering. Treverton did not build a programme and then invite people; it hosted a conversation and let the programme emerge from the professional network that formed around it. The association then provided continuity, peer review, and a standards framework for decades.

**VoG application.** Before the first teaching module is delivered, the Valley of Grace should host an equivalent founding gathering: a conference on place-based education and bioregional learning, held in the valley, inviting teachers from the ward's schools, CapeNature staff, EEASA members (the association still holds annual conferences), and the GoE network. The aim is not to present a finished programme. It is to constitute a professional community that will co-develop the programme. The 1982 model says this is the step that creates institutional durability. The October 2026 BioQuest cycle may be the moment.

### 5.4 Land tenure and conservation status matter

Treverton's environmental programmes rest on Trust-owned land. The Trust controls zoning, access, and land use. Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife's "Site of Conservation Significance" designation adds a statutory overlay that protects the wildlife area from future development pressure.

**VoG application.** The Valley of Grace has multiple land tenures: municipal commonage, TRANCRAA land (Farm 39), private farms, and the Greyton Nature Reserve (under CapeNature management). Each carries different constraints and opportunities. The Bioregional Learning Centre's physical site needs to be on land with a tenure arrangement that guarantees long-term educational access, whether through a trust, a lease, or a CapeNature partnership. The commonage is the obvious candidate, but only if its governance is settled. The PAMP alignment already documented in the Ward 2 High School Education Proposal provides the CapeNature hook.

### 5.5 Build external recognition into the design from day one

Treverton accumulated Eco-School status, the WESSA Corporate Award, and the Ezemvelo designation over decades. Each one became a ratchet preventing retreat. The Valley of Grace can compress this timeline by designing for recognition from the start.

**VoG application.** The GoE BioScore is already a form of external validation. The programme should also target WESSA Eco-School certification for participating schools, seek a partnership with EEASA (which would connect the programme to the very network Treverton helped create in 1982), and pursue formal recognition of the Bioregional Learning Centre as a PAMP partner under the Riviersonderend Complex management plan. Each recognition creates a stakeholder with a reason to defend the programme's continuity.

### 5.6 Permaculture and food systems as a curriculum bridge

Treverton's Stewardship Programme uses permaculture as the entry point for sustainability education: theory first, then raised-bed gardens, then broader resource-management projects. This is practical, visible, and produces food, which matters in a community where food security is a live concern.

**VoG application.** The Ward 2 Schools Program already references agricultural sciences and agroecology as optional contest streams. The Treverton model suggests promoting food systems from an optional stream to a core pathway. A permaculture garden at the Bioregional Learning Centre, managed by learners across the ward's schools, would serve as both a teaching facility and a demonstration site for the emerging producers' economy. This connects directly to the Genadendal Saturday Market and the valley's wider food-system aspirations.

### 5.7 The 40-year arc

Treverton's environmental identity took 40 years to build: from the 1982 EEASA conference to the current Stewardship Programme and Wacky Wednesdays. Each decade added a layer. The Wildlife Area came 17 years after the conference. The WESSA award came 13 years after the Wildlife Area. The current curriculum innovations are the most recent layer.

**VoG application.** The Valley of Grace 2038 strategy already thinks in generational time. The learning campus should be designed as a 40-year project, with explicit milestones. The 2028 anniversary of the mission station (290 years) and the 2038 tercentenary are already marked as significant dates. A learning campus launched in 2026-2027, reaching maturity by the mid-2060s, would follow the same generational arc as Treverton's environmental trajectory. The schools program documentation is right to start small (one subject, one grade, one BioQuest cycle). Treverton confirms that this is the correct tempo.

## 6. Key Differences and Cautions

The Treverton model is instructive but not directly transplantable. Three structural differences shape how the lessons apply.

**Private vs. public schools.** Treverton is a private boarding school serving approximately 400 students from families that pay fees. The Valley of Grace schools are public institutions in a community where youth unemployment exceeds 44 percent. The economic models are fundamentally different. Treverton's Trust-based funding cannot be replicated, but the BCU/Realm treasury model offers an alternative funding architecture that does not depend on school fees.

**Single campus vs. distributed valley.** Treverton's programmes work because everything is on one 270-hectare estate with a single governance structure. The Valley of Grace spans 10,000 hectares across multiple tenures, multiple settlements, and multiple schools. The Bioregional Learning Centre must function as a hub for a distributed network, not as a self-contained campus. This is a harder coordination problem.

**Heritage depth.** Genadendal carries 288 years of documented mission-station history, a Khoi sanctuary predating the mission, and the weight of apartheid removals and returns. Treverton's heritage is institutional (a Baptist school founded in the 1940s/1960s). The Valley of Grace's heritage dimension is far deeper and more politically charged. This is an asset for curriculum design, but it demands historical care that a nature-education model alone does not require.

## 7. Suggested Next Steps

1. Contact EEASA directly (eeasa.org.za) to explore a partnership or co-hosted event. The 1982 connection to Treverton gives the Valley of Grace a narrative entry point into an established professional network.

2. Approach Treverton College for a site visit and knowledge exchange. The school's marketing contact is marketing@treverton.co.za; phone +27 (0)33 263 1251. Two institutional histories (*Hope Beyond* and *Hope Beyond Expectations*) and Pat Irwin's EEASA 25-year review would be worth sourcing.

3. Design the Bioregional Learning Centre site plan with learning corridors, not only buildings. Treverton's spatial model says the campus must extend into the landscape. Map the corridors: commonage, Nature Reserve, Farm 39, riparian zones, food-forest sites.

4. Incorporate WESSA Eco-School certification and EEASA membership into the Ward 2 Schools Program design as explicit milestones.

5. Commission or identify a permaculture demonstration garden as part of the Bioregional Learning Centre's first-phase infrastructure.
