An integrated site, programme, and institutional design brief for the proposed Valley of Grace Learning Campus. Prepared for the W2DF working group as a foundation for the feasibility study (Action 0.5) and partnership approaches (Actions 0.7 to 0.9).

**This is a working document. It synthesises material from the [[learning-campus|option profile]], the [[learning-campus-actions|actions plan]], the [[learning-campus-economics|economics module]], and the [[context|Treverton research brief]]. It does not represent agreed community decisions. All claims carry the evidence standards from the Decision Tool: IC, MS, or TBV.**


## Part 1: Site design

### Principle: the campus is a landscape, not a site

The Treverton precedent (IC) demonstrates that environmental and place-based education becomes credible when the learning environment and the subject matter are the same place. Treverton's 270-hectare estate contains multiple ecosystems, a 30-hectare wildlife and wetland area managed by students, trail networks, and conventional school infrastructure. The campus is itself a landscape.

The Valley of Grace operates at a different scale. The [Riviersonderend Mountain Catchment Area](https://mghydro.com/app/shared/133FB4) applicable to our valley covers approximately 10,000 hectares across multiple land tenures: 
- municipal commonage (Erf 595, ~2,200 hectares), 
- the Greyton Nature Reserve (CapeNature), 
- Farm 39 (Genadendal) TRANCRAA land, 
- private farms, and 
- the Genadendal mission settlement. 

![[Screenshot 2026-09-17 at 14.43.59.png]]

The learning campus does not need to own or control this territory. It needs a base facility and defined learning corridors that extend into the landscape under agreements with the landholders.

The design implication: the campus is a node, not a compound. Buildings house classrooms, workshops, offices, and meeting space. The valley houses the curriculum.

### Learning corridors

Four corridors extend from the base facility into the surrounding landscape. Each serves specific curriculum areas and carries its own access and governance requirements.

**Corridor 1: Greyton commonage and Nature Reserve.** Fynbos ecology, alien vegetation clearing, water catchment, biodiversity monitoring (iNaturalist/BioScore). Access is governed by CapeNature (Nature Reserve) and TWKM (commonage) and the land is currently mostly stewarded by [Wild Restoration](https://www.wildrestoration.org). The [Riviersonderend Complex Protected Area Management Plan](https://www.capenature.co.za/uploads/files/Reports/Riviersonderend-Complex-PAMP.pdf) (PAMP) already identifies education and awareness as management objectives. Corridor 1 is the primary field site for Life Sciences, Geography, and the BLC's ecological programmes.

**Corridor 2: Genadendal heritage precinct.** Mission station heritage (founded 1738), the Genadendal Mission Museum, the historic church, the watermill, and the Khoi heritage landscape predating the mission. Access governed by the Genadendal community, the Moravian Church, and the Museum (Iziko/DCAS). Corridor 2 is the primary field site for History, Tourism Studies, and the BLC's heritage programmes. The 2038 tercentenary of the mission is a fixed milestone that shapes programming.

**Corridor 3: Agricultural landscape.** Commercial farms in the valley (stone fruit, wine grapes, proteas, livestock), the Genadendal commonage gardens, and the Farm 39 TRANCRAA land. Access governed by individual farm owners (through W2DF relationships) and, for Farm 39, the Communal Property Association and DALRRD. Corridor 3 is the primary field site for Agricultural Sciences and the skills centre's Elsenburg-linked programmes. Host farm placements for Elsenburg occupational qualifications use this corridor.

**Corridor 4: Riparian and wetland systems.** The Gobos River, its tributaries, and the wetland systems connecting the Greyton commonage to the Riviersonderend. Ecological monitoring, water quality, bioprecipitation research, and restoration work (erosion management, alien clearing along watercourses). Access depends on which properties the watercourses cross. Corridor 4 supports Geography, Life Sciences, and the BLC's research programmes. It is also the corridor with the strongest connection to the [Cape Water Performance-Based Bond](https://www.gov.za/news/media-statements/western-cape-treasury-rmb’s-launch-africa’s-first-nature-linked-outcomes)'s bankable services (water yield, ecosystem condition), making student monitoring data potentially relevant to bond reporting.

### Base facility: site options

The working group must resolve where the base facility sits. Three broad scenarios, each with different governance and perception implications.

**Scenario A: Genadendal.** Closest to the largest population in the ward. Places the institution on ground the Genadendal community identifies as its own. Carries the strongest signal that the campus serves the whole ward, starting from its most underserved settlement. Land tenure is complicated: the mission settlement sits on Moravian Church land, and surrounding areas involve TRANCRAA processes. The Hester Dorothea Conference Centre is a potential interim venue for Phase 1 programmes while a permanent facility is planned.

**Scenario B: Greyton.** Closer to the English-speaking families whose children form part of the high school target market. Risks being perceived as a Greyton initiative with Genadendal participation rather than a ward institution. Municipal commonage land (Erf 595) is the obvious candidate, but Erf 595 carries its own governance complications (see Greyton Commonage Research Report: the sale of the R100 school site on Erf 595 to a private buyer in 2013 remains contentious, and the commonage management framework under TWKM is not settled).

**Scenario C: Between the settlements.** A site on the R406 corridor between Greyton and Genadendal, or on Farm 39 land if the TRANCRAA process resolves. Geographically neutral. Requires new infrastructure (no existing buildings to repurpose). May lack the identity anchor that locating in either settlement provides.

The site decision is inseparable from the governance question (Part 3 below). The working group cannot resolve one without the other. The Treverton precedent (Lesson 5.4) confirms that land tenure and conservation status determine long-term institutional durability: the campus needs a site with a tenure arrangement that guarantees educational access over decades, not a year-to-year lease.

### Interim arrangements

Phase 1 programmes (CET satellite, first skills courses, BLC community education) do not require a purpose-built facility. They require a venue with classrooms, basic facilities, and access to at least one learning corridor. Three interim possibilities:

1. The Hester Dorothea Conference Centre in Genadendal: existing meeting and accommodation facilities, adjacent to the heritage precinct (Corridor 2). Requires agreement with the conference centre management (Moravian Church).
2. Existing community halls in Genadendal or Greyton: lower quality, but available for hire. Adequate for short courses and workshops. Also see [[properties/index|Available Properties]].
3. A commercial farm facility: several valley farms have conference or workshop spaces. Offers direct access to Corridor 3 but limits access to other corridors.

The interim venue is not the permanent site. It is the platform for Phase 1 while the working group resolves the permanent site question. Phase 1 programmes generate the track record and community buy-in that inform the site decision.


## Part 2: Programme design

### The three components

The campus combines three functions under one NPC. They share a site, governance, and (where appropriate) staff and infrastructure. They register separately under different authorities.

**Component 1: The high school.** Registered independent school, CAPS-compliant, English-medium, Grade 8 to 12. Small: 60 to 120 learners at maturity, starting Grade 8 only and growing one year group per year. Registered with [WCED](https://www.westerncape.gov.za/education) under [SASA Section 46](https://www.isasa.org/overview-of-independent-schools/). Examination body TBD ([SACAI](https://www.sacai.org.za) or [IEB](https://www.ieb.co.za)). The school's curriculum is bioregionally grounded: Geography uses the Riviersonderend watershed, History uses Genadendal's mission heritage, Life Sciences uses fynbos ecology, and elective subjects (Tourism, Agricultural Sciences, Business Studies) draw on the valley's actual economy. The small-school subject menu problem is addressed through integration with the skills centre.

**Component 2: The Bioregional Learning Centre (BLC).** Place-based education and research facility serving both the school and the wider community. The [BLC](https://bit.ly/BioLearnC) is the node from which the four learning corridors radiate. It develops the bioregional curriculum modules, runs community education programmes, hosts research, and operates heritage and ecological tours as a social enterprise. Learners take stewardship responsibility for allocated restoration blocks within the Realm: wetland rehabilitation, alien clearing, erosion management, and species monitoring. The conservation area's condition becomes part of their learning record.

The BLC is what makes the school distinctive and what makes the campus a destination. International precedents for bioregional learning centres include the [Schumacher College Small School](https://www.schumachercollege.org) (Devon, UK), the [Findhorn Foundation](https://www.findhorn.org) (Scotland), [SEKEM](https://sekem.com/en/cultural-life/education-schools/) (Egypt), and the [Sustainability Institute](https://www.sustainabilityinstitute.net/learning-at-the-si/) (Stellenbosch). Each operates at the intersection of education, ecology, and community economics. The Valley of Grace BLC is designed to join this network, not replicate any single model.

**Component 3: The community skills centre.** QCTO-accredited Skills Development Provider offering occupational qualifications, short courses, and learnerships aligned with the valley's economy. Serves adults and high school learners in integrated programmes. Not a [TVET college](https://www.westerncape.gov.za/further-education-and-training) (no DHET establishment required). Programme-specific accreditation from relevant SETAs: [CATHSSETA](https://www.cathsseta.org.za) (tourism and hospitality), [AgriSETA](https://www.agriseta.co.za) (agriculture), and others as demand emerges. A potential CET satellite learning centre (under the [WC CET College](https://wc.cetc.edu.za)) brings state-funded adult basic education at essentially no cost to the campus.

### Phasing and sequencing

The three components do not launch simultaneously. The phasing builds institutional capacity and demonstrates demand before committing to the most complex component.

**Phase 0 (November 2026 to mid-2027).** No programmes. Governance, partnerships, feasibility, site. The working group convenes, the Transformasie Komitee is engaged, accreditation timelines are mapped, and partnership approaches to the CET College, [Elsenburg](https://www.elsenburg.com/elsenburg-agricultural-training-institute/), and CATHSSETA are made. The NPC is registered. One early-harvest action: coordinate Elsenburg applications for the 2028 intake (applications open 1 April 2027), using W2DF farmer relationships to secure host farm placements. This requires no campus infrastructure.

**Phase 1 (mid-2027 to end of 2028).** Skills centre and BLC launch from an interim venue. CET satellite opens (if WC CET College agrees). First SETA-funded programmes: tourist guide training (CATHSSETA, 8 to 10 learners), agricultural occupational qualifications (Elsenburg partnership), basic business skills. BLC begins community education: heritage walks, ecological literacy, food sovereignty workshops. Revenue generation and track record building. Outcomes documented. [WESSA Eco-School](https://www.wessa.org.za/eco-schools/) certification and [EEASA affiliation](https://eeasa.org.za/home/membership/) pursued during this phase.

**Phase 2 (2028 to 2029).** High school registration application submitted to WCED (12 to 18 months before intended opening). Founding principal and first teachers recruited. Grade 8 cohort enrolled: 15 to 20 learners. From the first year, high school learners participate in BLC programmes.

**Phase 3 (2029 to 2033).** School grows by one year group per year. First matric cohort writes in 2032 or 2033. Skills centre expands based on Phase 1 demand data. BLC develops research programmes (2038 Genadendal tercentenary, leopard corridor, bioprecipitation) and international bioregional network connections.

### Curriculum integration

The campus model's core innovation is the integration between the high school and the skills centre. This must be designed, not improvised.

A Tourism learner in the high school does practical components through the CATHSSETA-accredited guide training programme running on the same campus. An Agricultural Sciences learner does practical blocks through the Elsenburg-linked programme, with host farm placements coordinated through the W2DF. A Business Studies learner participates in the campus commitment pool (see economics module) as a live business operation. The school's elective range is wider than its enrolment alone could support because adult skills programmes carry the infrastructure and staffing for those subjects.

Staff profiles reflect the integration. 
- A Tourism teacher who also facilitates adult guide training. 
- An Agriculture teacher who coordinates the Elsenburg programme. 
- A conservation specialist who runs BLC field programmes and teaches Life Sciences. 

Phase 1 is when these people are identified and, in some cases, developed.

### The two-economy curriculum

The economics module ([[learning-campus-economics|separate design document]]) describes how the campus teaches both the market economy (+M) and the [BioConomy](https://wiki.bioconomy.earth) (+E). Three operational elements require design attention in the brief:

**The campus commitment pool.** A pool among the campus community (students, staff, parents, skills centre participants, BLC volunteers) where members contribute and draw on commitments. Governed through four protocol functions: curation, valuation, limitation, exchange. Students who cannot pay full school fees in rand may contribute through the pool. This is not charity; it is a second coordination mechanism with its own accounting. The Transformasie Komitee and the W2DF working group are the governance bodies through which the pool's curation and limitation functions would operate. Feasibility TBV: no operating precedent at a South African school.

**Community exchange documentation.** Students in the BLC research component document existing reciprocity practices in the valley: rotating labour arrangements, informal childcare networks, material sharing, the stokvel system. This produces primary-source material for the economics curriculum and makes the +E layer visible to institutions that see only the +M layer.

**Ecological commitment accounting.** Conservation and alien clearing work by students is tracked in both registers: the rand cost of equivalent commercial clearing (for grant reporting and SETA compliance) and the commitment-pool contribution (for internal governance). This dual accounting makes visible the value of work that the market economy cannot price.

### Applying the Treverton lessons

Seven lessons from the Treverton research brief, applied to the programme design:

**1. Campus as landscape.** Addressed in Part 1. The four learning corridors extend the campus into the valley.

**2. Students govern, not only observe.** The BLC allocates restoration blocks to learners for ongoing stewardship. The campus commitment pool is governed by its members. Student governance is structural, not ceremonial.

**3. Start with a conference, not a curriculum.** Before the first teaching module is delivered, the campus should host a founding gathering on place-based education and bioregional learning. Invite teachers from the ward's schools, CapeNature staff, EEASA members, and the international bioregional network. The aim is to constitute a professional community that co-develops the programme.

**4. Land tenure matters.** Addressed in Part 1 (site options) and Part 3 (NPC structure).

**5. Build external recognition into the design from day one.** Phase 1 targets: WESSA Eco-School certification for participating schools and EEASA affiliation for the campus. Phase 3 targets: formal recognition of the BLC as a PAMP partner under the Riviersonderend Complex management plan. Each recognition creates a stakeholder with a reason to defend the programme's continuity. Recognition is a governance mechanism, not decoration (IC, from Treverton precedent).

**6. Permaculture and food systems as a curriculum bridge.** A permaculture garden at the BLC, managed by learners across the ward's schools, serves as teaching facility and demonstration site. Connects to the Greyton Saturday Market and the valley's food-system aspirations.

**7. The 40-year arc.** The campus is designed as a generational project. The 2038 Genadendal tercentenary is the first major milestone. A campus launched in 2027 and reaching maturity by the mid-2060s follows the same generational arc as Treverton's 40-year environmental trajectory. The correct tempo is small and deliberate.


## Part 3: Institutional architecture

### Legal structure

A single Non-Profit Company (NPC) registered with CIPC. The NPC holds:

- The school registration (WCED, SASA Section 46). 
- The Skills Development Provider accreditation (QCTO). 
- Programme-specific SETA accreditations (CATHSSETA, AgriSETA). 
- The CET satellite agreement (WC CET College). 
- Any leases, property agreements, or conservation partnerships. 
- NPO registration (Department of Social Development) and PBO registration (SARS) for Section 18A tax-deductible donations.

The Memorandum of Incorporation should be drafted with objects broad enough to cover education, training, research, tourism, and community development. This avoids having to amend the MOI as new programmes are added.

### Board composition

The board composition question is foundational. It determines whose institution this is perceived to be, and whose priorities shape its direction.

The working group must settle this before NPC incorporation (Action 0.12). A minimum viable board includes representatives from:

- Genadendal (Transformasie Komitee nominee or community representative). 
- Greyton (W2DF member or parent representative). 
- The farming community (commercial farmer representative, relevant for Elsenburg partnerships and host farm coordination). 
- Education sector (someone with school governance, SETA, or curriculum experience). 
- The broader ward (Genadendal, Bereaville, Voorstekraal, and surrounding farms).

The board should not be dominated by any single settlement or demographic. The campus serves the ward.

### Partnership architecture

The campus does not build capacity from scratch. It connects to existing institutions that already hold the accreditation, expertise, and infrastructure the campus needs.

### Accreditation pathway

Four separate accreditation processes run in parallel. Each has its own lead time and requirements.

**QCTO Skills Development Provider accreditation.** Required to deliver occupational qualifications. Application requires: quality management system, facilitator qualifications, assessment capacity, physical resources. Lead time: 6 to 12 months. Target: secured before Phase 1 launch.

**CATHSSETA training provider accreditation.** Programme-specific. Required to deliver tourist guide qualifications and claim learnership funding. Requires qualified facilitators, a venue, and an assessment system. The WCG DEDAT also has a formal application process for tourism training provider accreditation. Lead time: 6 to 12 months. Target: secured before Phase 1 launch.

**WCED independent school registration (SASA Section 46).** Required to operate the high school. Application requires: constitution, curriculum description (CAPS), premises description, governing body details, evidence of financial viability. Lead time: 12 to 18 months. Target: application submitted early 2028 for 2029 opening.

**CET satellite establishment.** Not an accreditation: the WC CET College holds the accreditation and extends it to the satellite. Requires a venue and a hosting agreement. Lead time: depends on the CET College's processes and willingness. Target: operational by mid-2027.

### Revenue model

No single revenue stream sustains the campus. The combined model draws from:

- School fees (Phase 2 onward). 
- SETA learnership and skills programme funding (Phase 1 onward). 
- CET College allocation for the satellite (Phase 1 onward, if established). 
- Grants and donor funding (TGF grant, Rotary, SEFA, conservation and heritage grants). 
- BLC tourism income (heritage and ecological tours, visiting groups, BioQuest events). 
- Corporate partnerships (Cape Water Bond alignment, Globeleq co-funding, CSI). 
- Campus commitment pool (supplementary, not primary).

The feasibility study (Action 0.5) must model minimum viable combined revenue against five-year operating costs. The study should also identify which revenue streams can begin before the school opens (SETA funding, CET allocation, grants, tourism income) and which depend on the school (fees).

### Recognition strategy

Following Treverton's Lesson 5.5, external recognition is built into the design from day one as a governance mechanism. Each recognition creates an external stakeholder with a reputational interest in the campus's continuity.

| Recognition | Target phase | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| WESSA Eco-School certification | Phase 1 | Establishes environmental education credentials; connects to national network |
| EEASA membership / affiliation | Phase 1 | Connects to the professional network Treverton helped create in 1982; provides peer review |
| PAMP partnership (Riviersonderend Complex) | Phase 1-2 | Formalises BLC's role in protected area education and monitoring |
| Ezemvelo-equivalent conservation status | Phase 3 | Designates campus restoration blocks as sites of conservation significance (KZN Wildlife model) |
| Bioregional learning network membership | Phase 2-3 | Connects to international precedents (Schumacher, Findhorn, SEKEM, Sustainability Institute) |
| GoE BioScore integration | Phase 1 | Student monitoring data feeds the Nature Realm's biodiversity accounting |

### Governance of the commitment pool

The campus commitment pool requires its own governance layer within the NPC structure. Two existing bodies provide the oversight:

- The Transformasie Komitee (representing the Genadendal community) contributes to the curation function: what commitments belong in the pool, and whose commitments are accepted?
- The W2DF working group (representing the ward-wide constituency) contributes to the limitation function: how much can any member contribute or withdraw before the balance shifts?

The pool's valuation and exchange functions are operational matters handled by the campus management, subject to the governance bodies' oversight.

This governance design is MS. No operating precedent exists at a South African school. The feasibility study should include a governance design component that tests the commitment pool concept with community members before launch.


## Part 4: Critical path and decision points

### What the working group must decide first

Three decisions gate everything else. They cannot be delegated to the feasibility study because the feasibility study's scope depends on them.

**Decision 1: Scope.** Is the working group exploring this concept, or committing to build it? Exploration commissions a feasibility study. Commitment commissions a feasibility study and begins partnership approaches in parallel. The actions plan assumes commitment, but the group must make this call explicitly.

**Decision 2: Site direction.** Not the final site selection (that requires the feasibility study and community process), but a direction: are we looking in Genadendal, in Greyton, or between the two? And are we willing to use an interim venue for Phase 1 while the permanent site is resolved? The interim venue question is separable from the permanent site question and can be answered sooner.

**Decision 3: Relationship to Silverwood.** Do the campus proponents and the Silverwood proponents see themselves as competing, complementary, or potentially merged? A direct conversation (Action 0.10) settles this before either initiative invests further energy.

### The critical path

W2DF champion appointed (November 2026) → working group convened → Transformasie Komitee engaged and site direction established (in parallel) → feasibility study and partnership approaches (in parallel) → NPC registered and seed funding secured → Phase 1 launch (mid-2027).

The Elsenburg coordination (Action 1.4) can run ahead of this entire path. Applications for the 2028 intake open 1 April 2027 and close 30 June 2027. If valley commercial farmers register as host farms and Elsenburg accepts applicants, the first tangible result of the campus concept arrives before the NPC exists.

### What has to be true for this to work

The campus model rests on five structural conditions. If any is absent, the model does not hold.

**Community legitimacy.** The Genadendal community, through the Transformasie Komitee, sees the campus as serving priorities it has already identified. Without this, the campus is an outsider initiative regardless of where it sits.

**Enough high school learners.** The feasibility study must confirm that 15 to 20 families will enrol children in Grade 8 at a new, unproven independent school. This is not guaranteed.

**Enough adults for skills programmes.** The skills centre needs a pipeline of adult learners for SETA-funded programmes. The CET satellite creates this pipeline (GETC completers become eligible for occupational qualifications), but the pipeline must exist before the school opens.

**Cross-component staffing.** Teachers who can work across the school and the skills centre. These profiles are unusual. Phase 1 is when they are found and tested.

**Sustained coordination.** The combined model is harder to run than any single component. It requires someone (not a volunteer committee, a paid coordinator or manager) who holds the relationships with SETA, CET College, Elsenburg, CapeNature, WCED, and the community simultaneously, over years.


## Evidence status

- **IC** (Independently Corroborated): confirmed through two or more independent sources, or through official records (legislation, WCED data, school websites, published research).
- **MS** (Mission-Sourced): comes from project documents. These describe plans, proposals, and local strategy. They are accurate descriptions of intention, not independent verification of outcomes.
- **TBV** (To Be Verified): the claim is plausible but has not been confirmed. The source is identified, and the verification step is named. Parents are encouraged to fill TBV gaps by checking directly with schools, providers, or authorities.
