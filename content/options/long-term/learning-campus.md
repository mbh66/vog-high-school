An integrated learning campus for Ward 2 with four parts on shared ground: an English-medium independent boarding school as anchor tenant, a public school of skills, a vocational training centre, and a Bioregional Learning Centre for post-school study and research. Emil Weder Secondary remains the valley's Afrikaans-medium public high school and is invited to use the campus as a partner. Not yet constituted. Earliest high school enrolment: 2030 (TBV). The vocational training centre and adult education could begin earlier.

**This profile is based on a feasibility exploration (MS) arising from the inaugural Ward 2 Business and Community Development Forum meeting (16 September 2026). It was revised on 23 September 2026 to add the anchor-tenant model and lessons from the Jakes Gerwel Technical School in Bonnievale. No formal proposal, registration, or funding application has been made. All claims about the campus are MS or TBV.**

## Objective

> By 2038, every young person in Ward 2 has a path from school into study or work in the valley, paid for in large part by families from outside it, and governed for the whole ward.

In full: the Valley of Grace Learning Campus brings the valley's schools together on shared ground. Emil Weder remains the valley's Afrikaans public high school. An English-medium boarding school rents space on the campus or establishes itself at the Uitkyk Hub, and its fees fund much of the campus's development. A school of skills and a vocational training centre prepare young people and adults for work in tourism and conservation. The Bioregional Learning Centre carries graduates of all of these into further study and research grounded in the valley. A trust representing the whole ward owns and governs the campus. By 2038, every path carries equal standing, and more of the valley's young people study, train, and work here.

The objective applies the Ward 2 Development Forum's long-term goal to education. The Forum wants every village to be able to say, by the 2038 tercentenary, that people work together, are treated as equals, and are free to learn, believe, and celebrate in their own way (MS). See https://df.valleyofgrace.co.za/priorities/education-skills

## The premise

Two findings shape the model.

First, a standalone English-medium day school is not viable. The valley has too few high-school-age learners to sustain the infrastructure, staffing, and fee levels a conventional independent school requires.

Second, a boarding school changes the numbers. It recruits from across South Africa and beyond, so its size does not depend on how many children live in the valley. If it leases its site from a community trust and pays a levy for each learner, its fees can fund much of the campus's development, including the parts that serve valley residents who could never pay those fees (MS).

The campus shares land, buildings, staff, and revenue across its parts, and serves the whole ward. It responds directly to the five development priorities of the Ward 2 Business and Community Development Forum: tourism, education and skills, agriculture and food sovereignty, conservation, and business development. See https://df.valleyofgrace.co.za/priorities/

Four of the five have an education or training component the valley currently cannot deliver locally. The boarding school also touches all five as an employer and buyer: parents visiting bring tourism, the kitchen buys valley food, the Bioregional Learning Centre works in the conservation estate, and supply contracts go to local businesses first (MS).

## What the campus contains

The parts share one site and one governing trust. Each registers separately with the authority that regulates it.

### The campus trust

A trust or non-profit company representing the whole ward holds the land and buildings and governs the campus. Its board includes Genadendal and Greyton representatives, and a Transformasie Komitee nominee if community land is involved. The boarding school operator is a tenant. It may hold a seat on the board, but never a controlling vote (MS).

This follows two things. At Bonnievale, a community trust holds half the seats on the school's governing body, and that stake is what gives the community ownership (see *Lessons from Bonnievale*). And under the design rules below, the boarding school's economic weight must not become governing power over the campus.

### The English-medium boarding school (anchor tenant)

A registered independent school, English-medium, Grade 8 to 12, with boarding. Run by an established school operator or a new operating company under a long lease from the trust. Size to be set by the feasibility study.

The lease commits the operator to:

- pay rent plus a levy per learner into a campus development fund
- reserve a fixed share of places for valley learners on bursary, drawn from every village
- hire and buy in the valley first: food, maintenance, transport, and services
- take part in the Bioregional Learning Centre's shared programme

All of these are proposals (MS). The terms are TBV through negotiation with an operator.

The curriculum is bioregionally grounded. Geography uses the Riviersonderend watershed. History uses Genadendal's mission heritage. Life Sciences uses fynbos ecology. Tourism, Agricultural Sciences, and Business Studies draw on the valley's actual economy. Learners taking Tourism do practical work through the vocational training centre's guide training. Learners taking Agricultural Sciences do practical blocks through the Elsenburg-linked programme.

### The school of skills

A public school offering a practical, occupation-focused curriculum. It sits on the same campus as the academic schools and shares their workshops, fields, and sports facilities, so the practical path carries equal standing. This is the main lesson from Bonnievale (see below). WCED admission rules and curriculum for schools of skills are TBV.

Two routes are possible (TBV with the WCED):

- a new public school of skills established by the WCED
- a Collaboration School or Donor-funded School under the Western Cape Provincial School Education Amendment Act of 2018, where a non-profit partner holds up to half the governing body seats and raises funds, and the state pays the teachers. The Supreme Court of Appeal upheld these models on 7 September 2026, dismissing a challenge by Equal Education and SADTU (reported by the George Herald; TBV against the judgment).

Whether the school of skills has any formal link to Emil Weder is for Emil Weder's governing body to decide.

### The vocational training centre

A QCTO-accredited Skills Development Provider offering occupational qualifications, short courses, and learnerships aligned with local industry needs, mainly tourism and conservation, and agriculture through Elsenburg. It serves adults (community members, emerging farmers, tourism entrants, school leavers), school of skills learners, and high school learners from the boarding school and Emil Weder in integrated programmes.

It does not need to be a TVET college, and it does not require DHET establishment. It operates under QCTO accreditation and seeks programme accreditation from the relevant SETAs: CATHSSETA (tourism and hospitality), AgriSETA (agriculture), and others as demand emerges.

A possible addition: a CET satellite learning centre under the Western Cape CET College's Overberg Community Learning Centre. It would bring state-funded adult basic education (AET Levels 1 to 4, GETC equivalent to Grade 9) to community members who did not complete school, and a route into the occupational qualifications.

Programmes mapped to the W2DF priorities include:

- tourist guide accreditation (cultural, nature, adventure)
- hospitality skills
- agricultural occupational qualifications (in partnership with Elsenburg)
- small-scale farmer development
- alien vegetation clearing and ecological restoration
- basic business management
- digital skills

Employers support the centre by pledging placements, mentors, equipment, and jobs. The valley has no single large employer like Bonnievale's Lactalis, so the pledges of many small tourism, farming, and conservation operators have to be pooled (MS). The boarding school, as the largest single employer on the campus, is the first pledger.

### The Bioregional Learning Centre (tertiary layer)

The post-school layer of the campus, and the part that gives all the others continuity. Graduates of Emil Weder, the boarding school, the school of skills, and the vocational training centre can all continue here into further study, research, and work in the valley.

It is designed as a node within the Valley of Grace Nature Realm (about 10,000 hectares), with learning corridors extending into the Greyton commonage, the Greyton Nature Reserve, and the Farm 39 TRANCRAA land. The centre is the base; the valley is the campus. It develops the bioregional curriculum, runs community education (heritage walks, ecological literacy, food sovereignty workshops), hosts research (the 2038 Genadendal tercentenary, leopard corridor mapping, bioprecipitation, BioConomy documentation), and runs heritage and ecological tours as a social enterprise. Learners from every school on the campus take stewardship of restoration blocks in the Realm: wetland rehabilitation, alien clearing, erosion management, and species monitoring.

Its tertiary role starts small (MS):

- occupational qualifications at higher NQF levels through the QCTO
- short courses and field programmes for visiting students and professionals
- degree-level study and research through university partners, such as Stellenbosch University or Rhodes University's Environmental Learning Research Centre through the EEASA connection (TBV)

Registration as a private higher education institution with the DHET is a long-term option only.

The centre works alongside the Genadendal Mission Museum and CapeNature, drawing on both.

### Emil Weder Secondary (partner)

Emil Weder remains the valley's Afrikaans-medium public high school, with its own heritage, governing body, and identity. It is not part of the campus. It is invited to use the vocational training centre and the Bioregional Learning Centre, and to take part in the shared place-based programme. Any closer link is Emil Weder's decision.

## How the money flows

- Boarding fees go to the operator. The operator pays rent and a levy per learner into the campus development fund.
- The state pays teachers at the school of skills (WCED), CET educators (CET College), and learnership costs (SETAs).
- Employers pledge placements, mentors, equipment, and jobs.
- Community-wide giving, grants, and CSI funding raise the capital for buildings.
- The Bioregional Learning Centre earns from tourism, research partnerships, and courses.

The development fund pays for valley bursaries at the boarding school, the vocational training centre, the Bioregional Learning Centre, and shared facilities (MS). None of this has been costed.

Dependence on one anchor tenant is the main financial risk. The public parts need their own funding base, and the lease should build a reserve, so that the campus survives if the operator struggles or leaves.

## Design rules: the three spheres

The Ward 2 Development Forum sorts the valley's life into three spheres, each with its own guiding principle. The campus applies them to itself (MS).

- **Cultural (freedom).** Each school keeps its own identity, language, and ethos: Emil Weder its Afrikaans and Moravian heritage, the boarding school its own character, the school of skills its practical focus. The Bioregional Learning Centre offers a shared place-based programme to all of them. The community sets the campus's purpose. No school sets the culture of another.
- **Civic (equality).** The trust holds the land and buildings for the whole ward. The boarding school is a tenant without a controlling vote. Every valley learner has equal access to the vocational training centre and the Bioregional Learning Centre. A fixed share of boarding places goes to valley learners from every village. No path is second class.
- **Economic (cooperation).** The boarding school pays rent and a levy, hires locally, and buys from valley suppliers first. Employers pledge placements and jobs. Residents give what they can. Industry advises the vocational training centre on what skills are needed. It does not decide what the campus is for.

## Lessons from Bonnievale

Jakes Gerwel Technical School in Bonnievale opened in 2016 after community leaders found that most Grade 7 learners in the town had to leave for high school (IC). It is a no-fee public school under the Western Cape's Collaboration Schools model, with a community trust holding half the governing body seats (IC). According to the IPASA case study (2023), the WCED put R54 million into construction, the community raised about R100 million in cash and kind over three and a half years, and nearly 700 learners are enrolled. The case study describes it as the only school in the country offering mainstream academic subjects and the School of Skills curriculum on one campus. The trust raises about R1.8 million a year to cover the school's budget gap. Bonnievale 418, a non-profit set up in 2021, extends the work into early childhood development, foster care, housing, and youth employment, with a target of zero youth unemployment by 2030. These figures are single-source (TBV). The ASLA Foundation supports technical training at the school and has renovated two residences, Kruispad Huise, as safe homes for vulnerable learners.

Two valley residents have been developing the school of skills and vocational training concept with early support from the team that set up the Jakes Gerwel school. They will be named here once they agree (MS).

Six lessons carry over to the Learning Campus:

1. **One campus for both paths.** Put the practical path on the same campus as academic learning, sharing facilities, so neither is seen as the lesser option.
2. **A formal community stake in governance.** Bonnievale's trust holds half the governing body seats. Here, the campus trust holds the land and governs the campus.
3. **A partnership with the state for the public parts.** The Collaboration Schools model lets the WCED pay teachers while a non-profit partner raises the rest.
4. **Community-wide fundraising.** Bonnievale's campaign drew small gifts from across the town. The breadth of giving built ownership as much as the money did.
5. **A wraparound body with one measurable target.** Bonnievale 418 aims for zero youth unemployment by 2030. The Learning Campus equivalent: every school leaver in Ward 2 in work, study, or training within a year of leaving school, by 2038.
6. **An anchor that buys and hires.** Bonnievale had a large local employer. The Learning Campus's anchor is the boarding school, as employer, buyer, and funder. Bonnievale's residences also show that boarding can serve vulnerable valley learners as well as fee-paying ones.

## Timeline

The campus does not launch all its parts at once. The phasing builds institutional capacity and demonstrates demand before committing to the most expensive parts (buildings and the boarding school).

**Phase 0 (now to mid-2027):** Convene a working group through the W2DF. Engage the Transformasie Komitee and Emil Weder's governing body. Visit Bonnievale. Run a feasibility study that tests demand for high-end boarding in the valley. Start the search for a boarding school operator. Approach the WCED about a school of skills, and the CET College, Elsenburg, and CATHSSETA about partnerships. Identify a site. Register the campus trust.

**Phase 1 (2027 to 2028):** Launch the CET satellite and the first SETA-funded programmes at the vocational training centre, in interim premises. Begin community education through the Bioregional Learning Centre. Sign heads of agreement with an operator. Launch the capital campaign. Pursue WESSA Eco-School certification and EEASA affiliation.

**Phase 2 (2028 to 2030):** Build or refurbish campus buildings. The operator registers the boarding school with the WCED. Establish the school of skills with the WCED. Open with Grade 8 at both schools, growing one year group at a time.

**Phase 3 (2030 to 2038):** Both schools grow to Grade 12. First matric cohort around 2034 (TBV). The vocational training centre runs multiple programmes each year. The Bioregional Learning Centre begins its tertiary programmes and hosts research, visitors, and tourism. The 2038 tercentenary is the point at which the objective is assessed.

A family making a 2028 decision cannot enrol a child in this school. A family whose child enters Grade 8 in 2028 elsewhere should know that the vocational training centre and Bioregional Learning Centre may be running before their child finishes school.

## Relationship to other options

**Emil Weder:** Emil Weder keeps its Afrikaans heritage and its place as the valley's public high school. The campus adds what Emil Weder cannot offer alone: practical pathways, the Bioregional Learning Centre, and post-school continuity. Emil Weder learners are invited to use both.

**Silverwood:** Silverwood is proposed as a school only. Its backers could become the boarding school operator, run a separate school, or step back. Which of these applies is an open question for a direct conversation.

**Online schooling and microschool:** During Phases 0 and 1, families using online schooling or a microschool from 2028 could use the vocational training centre and the Bioregional Learning Centre as supplementary resources, building the partial-option layer the Decision Tool anticipates.

## Best fit

The boarding school suits families, from inside or outside the valley, who want English-medium schooling grounded in the valley's ecology, heritage, and economy, and who can pay high-end fees or qualify for a valley bursary. The school of skills and vocational training centre suit learners and adults who want a practical path into tourism, conservation, or agriculture. The Bioregional Learning Centre suits school leavers from any of the valley's schools who want to keep studying close to home.

It suits the Genadendal community if the governance is right: the trust represents the ward, the practical path has equal standing, and adult education and jobs arrive early.

It does not suit families who need a school operating by 2028, or families who want a proven institution with a matric track record before enrolling. It carries all the risks of a start-up institution, compounded by the complexity of a combined model.

## Key unknowns

- **Demand for high-end boarding in the valley.** No one has tested whether families will pay high-end fees to board in Greyton or Genadendal. **TBV** through the feasibility study.
- **An operator.** Whether an established school group, or a new operator, would take a long lease on these terms (rent, levy, bursary places, local procurement, no controlling vote). **TBV** through operator approaches.
- **Capital.** The cost of buildings has not been estimated. Bonnievale needed state capital and three and a half years of community fundraising. **TBV** through the feasibility study.
- **The school of skills route.** Whether the WCED would establish a school of skills here, and whether a Collaboration or Donor-funded School is the better route. **TBV** with the WCED.
- **Emil Weder.** Whether Emil Weder's governing body wants to use the campus, and on what terms. **TBV** through the working group.
- **Genadendal representation.** Whether the W2DF working group can be convened with adequate Genadendal representation. The protocol concern raised at the inaugural meeting is foundational. **TBV** through the November 2026 W2DF meeting.
- **The CET satellite.** Whether the Western Cape CET College would host a satellite learning centre in Genadendal. **TBV** with the CET College.
- **Elsenburg.** Whether Elsenburg would partner on satellite delivery of agricultural qualifications, with valley farmers as host farms. **TBV** with Elsenburg and local farmers.
- **CATHSSETA.** Whether accreditation as a training provider is obtainable for a new organisation. **TBV** with CATHSSETA and the Western Cape Government.
- **Tertiary partners.** Whether a university would partner with the Bioregional Learning Centre. **TBV**.
- **Site.** Where the campus sits determines whose institution it is seen to be. No site has been identified. **TBV** through community process.
- **Silverwood.** **TBV** through direct conversation with Silverwood's proponents.
- **TRANCRAA and Farm 39.** If the campus involves community land or water rights, the Communal Property Association and the TRANCRAA process become central. **TBV** with the Transformasie Komitee and DALRRD.
