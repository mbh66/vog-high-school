The previous variables deal in facts that can be looked up: fees, subject lists, examination bodies, distances. This one deals in things harder to measure but no less real: who your child spends their days with, what social world they enter, and what relationship they maintain with the valley they live in.

## Peer group and friendships

The transition from primary to high school is a social rupture. Children who have spent seven years together at Greyton House Primary or Genadendal Primary are scattered by the high school decision. Some go to Caledon. Some go online. Some stay at Emil Weder. If Silverwood opens, some go there. The peer group fragments.

For some children, this is fine. For others, it is the hardest part of the transition, harder than the academic step-up or the longer school day. Parents whose children have close friendships in the valley should consider what happens to those friendships under each option.

- **Emil Weder:** The child joins the Genadendal peer group. If the child is from an English-speaking Greyton family, they may be one of very few English speakers in the school. Social integration depends on the child's personality, their Afrikaans competence, and how the school community receives them.
- **Overberg High School (Caledon):** The child joins a Caledon peer group. Daily friendships form around the school, not the valley. After-school socialising, birthday parties, and weekend activities shift to Caledon. The child's social centre of gravity moves 35 km away.
- **Online school:** The child has no school peer group in the physical sense. Social interaction happens through the platform's community features (if any), through extracurricular activities arranged separately, and through whatever the family and valley provide. This is the option that places the greatest burden on the family to actively construct a social life for the child.
- **Silverwood (if constituted):** If enough Greyton families enrol, the peer group stays local. The child walks to school with children from their own village. This is the strongest argument for a local school, and it is the one most often understated in debates that focus on curriculum and fees.
- **Boarding school:** The child enters the social world of the boarding school. Friendships form there. The child returns to the valley for holidays, increasingly as a visitor rather than a resident. This is not inherently negative, but parents should recognise it as a trade-off.

## Diversity and social composition

The valley holds sharp demographic contrasts. Greyton's permanent resident population is older, predominantly English-speaking, and relatively affluent. Genadendal's population is younger, predominantly Afrikaans-speaking, and economically constrained. Farm 39 is TRANCRAA land with unresolved tenure. Heuwelkroon is receiving 548 new subsidised housing units. These communities coexist in a single ward but do not always coexist in single institutions.

Each schooling option produces a different social composition:

- **Emil Weder** draws primarily from the Genadendal community. A Greyton child attending Emil Weder would experience a level of racial, linguistic, and economic diversity that no other option on this list provides. For some families, this is precisely the experience they want for their child. For others, the language barrier makes it impractical regardless of the social value.
- **A Greyton-based independent school** would likely draw from English-speaking families with the means to pay fees. The social composition would be narrower. This is a structural feature of independent schools in South Africa, not a criticism of any particular school's intentions. The expensive schools research in the project documents traces this pattern across the country.
- **Online schooling** has no local social composition at all. The child's peers are whoever else is on the platform, distributed nationally.
- **Caledon schools** serve a wider catchment than the valley alone. The social mix depends on the school: Overberg High School's Quintile 5 status suggests a fee-paying, mixed-income intake; Hoërskool Overberg's composition would differ again.

Parents who value diversity as an educational experience should recognise that the most diverse option (Emil Weder) is also the one with the highest language barrier for English-speaking families. The tool does not resolve this tension. It names it.

## Connection to place

The Ward 2 Schools Program and the BioHub architecture are built on a specific proposition: that education grounded in the learner's own valley produces a qualitatively different outcome from education grounded in a generic textbook. A child who studies the Riviersonderend watershed in Geography, the Khoi sanctuary history in History, and fynbos ecology in Life Sciences develops a relationship with their home environment that a child studying the same subjects from a Caledon or Cape Town classroom does not.

This matters for families who intend to stay in the valley and whose children may return after tertiary study. It matters less for families whose children will leave and not return.

The tool does not assume one orientation is better. It asks parents to be clear about their own.

- **Emil Weder and Silverwood** are the only options physically located in the valley and eligible for the Ward 2 Schools Program's curriculum modules.
- **Caledon schools** are outside the valley. The connection to Greyton and Genadendal's specific ecology, history, and economy is weaker.
- **Online schools** are locationless. The child can study about the valley through GoE and citizen science activities outside school hours, but the curriculum itself does not connect them to it.
- **Boarding schools** remove the child from the valley entirely for the duration of term.

## Community capacity

There is a collective dimension to this decision that individual families often miss. If 20 families each make separate decisions, the result is fragmentation: three children at Emil Weder, five in Caledon, seven online, five at boarding schools. No critical mass forms anywhere. No collective infrastructure (transport, microschool, new school) becomes viable.

If those same 20 families coordinate, the picture changes. Twenty children committed to a local option is enough to justify a dedicated school (Silverwood), a microschool with a full-time facilitator, or a subsidised transport service to Caledon. The planning session Michael is convening exists in part to surface this collective arithmetic.

The tool should help families see that their individual decision has a collective consequence, and that collective action may open options that individual action cannot.

## Questions to settle before moving on

1. How important is it to you that your child's school friends live in the valley?
2. Do you value a diverse school community, and if so, are you prepared for the trade-offs (language barrier, distance) that the most diverse options carry?
3. Do you want your child's education connected to the valley's ecology, heritage, and economy, or is a generic curriculum acceptable?
4. Would you participate in a collective arrangement (lift club, microschool, new school enrolment commitment) if enough families joined?
5. How many other families in Greyton are making this decision for 2028? Have you talked to them?
