Most parents think about this variable first and confront it last. The question is: what is the high school supposed to prepare your child for?

The answer shapes everything upstream. A family aiming at medical school needs Mathematics and Physical Sciences at a school that can teach them well. A family whose child will enter the local tourism economy needs something different. A family that does not yet know needs a school that keeps doors open.

## The pathways

### University (bachelor's degree)

Requires an NSC with a Bachelor pass: a minimum of 50% in four subjects and 30% in two others (beyond Life Orientation), with at least four subjects from the designated list. In practice, competitive programmes (medicine, engineering, actuarial science, law at top institutions) require 70% to 80%+ in prerequisite subjects.

The school's role: produce strong marks in the right subjects. This pathway favours schools with experienced teachers, rigorous assessment, and a culture of academic preparation. It favours Mathematics over Mathematical Literacy, and it requires that the school offers the subjects the target programme demands.

Universities do not discriminate by examination body (DBE, IEB, SACAI all carry equal weight for admission). They do not discriminate by school type (public, independent, online). They assess the qualification, the subject marks, and the APS.

Some universities require or recommend the National Benchmark Tests (NBTs), written separately from the NSC. Learners from any school type can register to write NBTs.

**For valley families:** University attendance means leaving the valley. The child will relocate to Cape Town, Stellenbosch, or further afield. The financial implication extends beyond school fees to university tuition, accommodation, and living costs. NSFAS (the National Student Financial Aid Scheme) funds qualifying students at public universities and TVET colleges. Bursaries from corporates, professional bodies, and university-specific funds are also available. The high school's role in university readiness includes helping the child navigate applications, financial aid, and the transition.

### TVET college (vocational qualification)

TVET colleges accept students who have completed Grade 9, 10, 11, or 12. The programmes lead to National Certificate Vocational (NCV) qualifications (Levels 2 to 4) or NATED (Report 191) diplomas (N4 to N6). Fields include engineering, hospitality and tourism, business management, IT, agriculture, and early childhood development.

**Boland TVET College** operates a campus in Caledon, approximately 35 km from Greyton. Programmes at the Caledon campus include Office Administration, Management, Tourism, and ECD. Engineering and agricultural technology are offered at the Worcester and Strand campuses.

The TVET route is undervalued by many parents who grew up regarding it as a fallback. The reality is different. TVET graduates in fields like electrical engineering, plumbing, welding, and hospitality enter the labour market faster than most university graduates and often earn immediately. In a valley where construction, agriculture, tourism, and food service are the core economic activities, TVET qualifications align closely with the local economy.

NSFAS funding is available for TVET students.

**For valley families:** A child completing Grade 9 or Grade 12 at any school in the valley or Caledon can apply to the Boland TVET Caledon campus. The TVET pathway does not depend on which high school the child attended, provided they meet the entry requirements for their chosen programme. A child who completes Grade 9 can enter an NCV Level 2 programme directly. A child who completes Grade 12 (NSC) enters NATED programmes at N4.

### Local economy entry

Some school leavers will enter the valley's economy directly: working in tourism (accommodation, guiding, hospitality), agriculture (farming, food production, food forests), construction, services, or starting a small enterprise. The Ward 2 Business Forum, the proposed Producers' Cooperative, and the GoE Nature Realm create economic infrastructure that did not exist five years ago.

For this pathway, the high school's contribution is less about marks and more about capability: communication, numeracy, practical skills, knowledge of the local economy, entrepreneurial orientation, and the confidence to participate. The Ward 2 Schools Program is explicitly designed to prepare learners for this pathway by grounding CAPS subjects in the valley's own economy and ecology.

A child who takes Tourism, Business Studies, and Agricultural Sciences at matric level and participates in the Schools Program's BioQuest and heritage evidence work leaves school with a direct understanding of the valley's economic architecture. This is a legitimate post-school pathway, and the tool should name it as one without implying it is a consolation prize.

### Gap year or international pathway

Some families plan for a gap year (structured or informal) before tertiary study. Others aim for international university applications (UK, Europe, North America). The NSC is accepted internationally, though some institutions require additional standardised tests (SAT, IELTS, TOEFL). Cambridge qualifications (if the child follows that pathway) may simplify applications to UK universities.

For valley families planning an international pathway, the high school's role is to produce strong academic results and help the child assemble an application that includes extracurricular depth, community engagement, and (for US applications) a compelling personal narrative. The GoE citizen science work and heritage evidence programme could serve as a distinctive element in such applications.

## The question most parents avoid

Many parents default to "university" as the target pathway without asking whether it is the right one for their specific child. South Africa produces more university applicants than places each year, and graduate unemployment is a real phenomenon. At the same time, artisans, technicians, and skilled trades workers are in short supply.

The decision tool does not rank these pathways. It asks parents to be honest about which one their child is suited for, which one the family can fund, and which one the available school options actually prepare for. A child who is practically inclined, who thrives in hands-on work, and who struggles with abstract academic content may be better served by a TVET pathway than by five years of frustration in a school optimised for university entrance.

The best position is a school that keeps multiple doors open through Grade 10 and 11, while helping the child and family clarify direction. The worst position is a school that closes doors early (by not offering Mathematics, or by not preparing learners for the subjects that university programmes require) without the family realising it.

## Questions to settle before moving on

1. Do you have a specific post-school pathway in mind for your child?
2. If university: which field? Does the school you are considering offer the prerequisite subjects?
3. If TVET: has your child considered the programmes at Boland TVET Caledon? Entry is possible from Grade 9.
4. If local economy: does the school connect your child to the valley's economic reality, or does it abstract them from it?
5. If you do not yet know: does the school keep doors open by offering Mathematics (not only Maths Literacy) and a reasonable range of electives?
