Not all schools are the same kind of institution, and the differences carry real consequences for the qualification your child receives, the fees you pay, and the protections you have if something goes wrong.

## Why it matters

South Africa's education system distinguishes sharply between public schools, registered independent schools, and home education. Each operates under different legislation, different fee structures, and different accountability mechanisms. A parent choosing between these categories is choosing between fundamentally different relationships with the state, the school, and the curriculum.

For valley parents, this variable matters because several of the available options sit in different categories: Emil Weder and Overberg High are public schools; Silverwood (if constituted) would be a registered independent school; online providers are registered with independent examination bodies; and a microschool or learning pod may or may not have any formal registration at all.

## The categories

### Public school (government school)

**What it is.** A school established and funded by the provincial education department (WCED in the Western Cape). Governed by a school governing body (SGB) composed of elected parents, teachers, and (at high school level) learners. The principal is a WCED employee. Teachers are employed either by the WCED (state-paid posts) or by the SGB (governing body posts funded from school fees).

**Fees.** Public schools are classified by quintile (1 to 5) based on the poverty level of the surrounding community. Quintile 1 to 3 schools are no-fee schools. Quintile 4 and 5 schools may charge fees, set by the SGB and approved by a majority of parents at an annual budget meeting. Parents who cannot afford fees may apply for exemption. Overberg High School in Caledon is classified as Quintile 5, which means it is fee-paying, but the fee amount is set locally and is orders of magnitude lower than independent school fees.

**Curriculum.** The Curriculum and Assessment Policy Statement (CAPS), mandated by the Department of Basic Education. The school has no discretion to deviate from CAPS content, although teaching methodology is at the teacher's and school's discretion.

**Examination.** The National Senior Certificate (NSC), administered by the DBE and quality-assured by Umalusi. This is the standard matric qualification. Universities, TVET colleges, and employers recognise it without question.

**Accountability.** The WCED oversees the school through circuit managers. The SGB is accountable to the parent body. The school must comply with the South African Schools Act, the Employment of Educators Act, and provincial regulations. Parents have recourse to the WCED, the MEC for Education, and ultimately the courts if governance fails.

**Local examples.** Emil Weder Secondary (Genadendal). Overberg High School and Hoërskool Overberg (Caledon).

### Registered independent school

**What it is.** A school registered with the provincial education department under Section 46 of the South African Schools Act. Registration is compulsory; operating an unregistered school is a criminal offence. The school is privately owned and governed (by a board of directors, a trust, or an NPC). It sets its own fees, hires its own staff, and may adopt its own pedagogical approach, provided it meets minimum standards.

**Fees.** Set by the school. No quintile classification. No fee exemption obligation (though some schools offer bursaries voluntarily). The range in the Western Cape runs from roughly R20,000 per year for a low-fee independent school to over R200,000 per year for a high-fee school (day attendance), with boarding adding R100,000 to R250,000 on top.

**Curriculum.** An independent school must offer a curriculum that meets the standards of the relevant examination body it is registered with. Most South African independent schools follow CAPS (examined by SACAI or IEB) or Cambridge International. Some follow both. The school has more freedom in how it teaches than a public school, and some (like a Waldorf school) adopt pedagogical approaches that differ significantly from mainstream practice while remaining curriculum-compliant.

**Examination.** Depends on the school's registration:
- **IEB** (Independent Examinations Board): writes the NSC through IEB. The qualification is identical in status to the DBE-administered NSC. Widely regarded as rigorous. Most high-fee independent schools use IEB.
- **SACAI** (South African Comprehensive Assessment Institute): writes the NSC through SACAI. Also Umalusi-accredited. Used by many online and distance providers, and by some smaller independent schools.
- **Cambridge International**: the school offers IGCSEs (Grade 10) and AS/A Levels (Grades 11-12). These are international qualifications. South African universities accept them for admission, but the conversion to an Admission Points Score (APS) follows specific rules. Parents choosing Cambridge should confirm that their target university or programme accepts the qualification.

**Accountability.** The WCED registers and may inspect the school. The examination body quality-assures the assessment. But an independent school is not governed by an elected parent SGB in the same way a public school is. The school's governing structure (board, trust, NPC) determines how much voice parents have. Parents who disagree with the school's direction may have limited formal recourse beyond withdrawing their child.

**Local relevance.** Silverwood School, if constituted, would fall into this category. The BioHub Founding Brief describes it as CAPS-compliant and Waldorf-inspired with project-based learning. Its registration status, examination body, and governing structure are not yet public. Parents should ask, before enrolling:

1. Is the school registered with the WCED?
2. Which examination body is it registered with (IEB, SACAI, or Cambridge)?
3. What is the governing structure and how are parents represented?
4. What is the fee, and is there a bursary or sliding-scale provision?
5. What is the minimum enrolment needed for the school to remain viable, and what happens if that number is not reached mid-year?

### Online/distance school

**What it is.** A school that delivers instruction through digital platforms. The child studies from home (or any location with internet access). Some providers offer live classes on a timetable; others deliver recorded lessons and structured materials for self-paced study. A few combine both.

**Registration.** Online schools are not registered with the WCED in the same way a physical school is. They register with an examination body (typically SACAI or IEB) as a distance education provider. The examination body, not the WCED, is the primary quality assurance mechanism. Parents should verify that the provider is registered with a Umalusi-accredited examination body. A provider that is not registered with SACAI, IEB, or the DBE cannot issue a National Senior Certificate.

**A critical distinction.** Online schools registered with SACAI or IEB do issue the NSC, but the NSC is issued by the examination body, not by the online school itself. The practical effect is the same: the learner receives a matric certificate. But parents should understand that the online school is a registered provider, not a registered school in the SASA sense.

**SBA requirements.** School-Based Assessments (SBAs) are a compulsory component of the NSC. Online schools must manage SBA submission and moderation through their examination body. Some subjects (physical sciences, life sciences) have practical components that require supervised laboratory work or equivalent arrangements. Parents should ask how the provider handles practicals for subjects that require them.

**Accountability.** The examination body is the primary regulator. Umalusi oversees the examination body. There is no SGB, no WCED circuit manager, and no annual parent budget meeting. If the provider fails or underperforms, the parent's recourse is to the examination body and, in extreme cases, to Umalusi. Withdrawing and re-enrolling elsewhere mid-year is possible but disruptive.

### Microschool or learning pod

**What it is.** A group of families pooling resources to educate their children together, typically with a shared tutor, a hired teacher, or a structured online curriculum supplemented by in-person facilitation. This model has grown internationally and has a small but growing presence in South Africa.

**Registration.** This is where the legal ground gets uncertain. Under South African law, a child of compulsory school age must either attend a registered school or be registered for home education with the provincial education department. A microschool that operates as a group of individually registered home-education families, each registered with the WCED and using an accredited curriculum provider, is legally compliant. A microschool that presents itself as a school without WCED registration is not.

The practical test: each child's parent must be registered as the home educator with the WCED, and the curriculum and assessment must run through a Umalusi-accredited examination body. The shared physical space and the tutor are support structures, not the legal schooling arrangement.

**Fees.** Depends entirely on the arrangement. A group of six families sharing a tutor at R15,000/month, plus individual online school fees of R2,000/month per child, works out to R4,500 per child per month. Economies of scale improve with more families, but so does the coordination burden.

**Accountability.** Minimal formal oversight. The WCED checks on registered home-education families (inspections are infrequent but legally possible). The examination body oversees assessments. The tutor or facilitator has no formal regulatory standing. This model depends almost entirely on parental commitment and the quality of the people involved.

**Local relevance.** If the valley's catchment area cannot support a conventional school, a microschool model combined with an accredited online curriculum could serve a small cohort of English-medium families. It would require a physical space, a committed group of parents, a competent facilitator, and a clear legal structure. It is the lowest-infrastructure option that keeps children physically together in the valley.

## The registration question for new schools

Any proposal for a new school in the valley (including Silverwood) must clear a registration threshold with the WCED. The WCED registration process for an independent school requires:

1. A completed application with the school's constitution, curriculum details, and governance structure.
2. Proof that the premises meet health and safety standards.
3. Evidence that the school will offer a curriculum at least equivalent to CAPS standards.
4. Registration with an examination body.

The WCED may take several months to process registration. A school that begins operating before registration is granted is operating illegally. Parents enrolling a child in an unregistered school risk the child's year not being recognised for progression or certification purposes.

## Questions to settle before moving on

1. Do you understand which category each option you are considering falls into?
2. For any independent or online school: which examination body is it registered with, and is that body Umalusi-accredited?
3. For Silverwood specifically: has it been registered with the WCED? If not, what is the expected timeline?
4. If you are considering a microschool or learning pod: have you registered for home education with the WCED, and are you using an accredited curriculum provider?
5. How much does formal accountability matter to you? A public school has layers of oversight that an online provider or a microschool does not.
