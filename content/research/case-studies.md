# Communities That Built Their Own Schools: Case Studies and Operational Best Practices

## TL;DR
- The most transferable models for our valley are not the celebrity or investor schools but community trusts that started small, registered as non-profit companies, and used sliding-scale fees plus donor bridging: 
	- Zithulele Independent School (Eastern Cape, 2016), 
	- the parent-founded Waldorf schools in Hermanus and Kommetjie, and 
	- LEAP Science and Maths Schools (2004) are the closest South African analogues, alongside 
	- Lagan College (Northern Ireland, 1981) as the best cross-community precedent.
- The single biggest risk to a new Western Cape independent school in its first three years is regulatory: failure to register educators with SACE, delays in Umalusi accreditation, unapproved premises or zoning, and thin finances that force the hiring of unqualified teachers. These are the exact reasons the WCED moved to withdraw registration from schools such as Darul Islam Islamic High School.
- Decide early between two legal routes: an independent school (a non-profit company plus governing trust, registered with WCED, accredited by Umalusi, eligible for a state subsidy of up to 60% of the provincial per-learner cost if fees stay low), or a Section 21 public school on community-provided land. Independent gives control and speed; public gives funded teacher posts but slower state delivery.

## Key Findings
- Community-initiated schools that survive share four features: a legal vehicle that separates founder vision from day-to-day governance (a trust or non-profit company with a representative board), a blended funding model that never depends on fees alone in the first five years, a deliberate mechanism to hold richer and poorer families in one institution (sliding-scale fees), and an explicit community-benefit mandate beyond its own enrolment.
- South African low-fee "network" schools (SPARK, Nova Pioneer) were investor-founded and are weak models for genuine community instigation, though their financial and teacher-training practices are instructive.
- High schools are harder to found than primary schools everywhere in the evidence. Many community schools start with foundation phase grades and add secondary years one grade at a time, which is how Imhoff, Streetlight and the Māori kura reached matric level.
- Cross-community schools serving a class or ethnic divide (Lagan College, the Hermanus Waldorf School, Zithulele) succeed when the mixing is designed into admissions, fees and language policy from day one rather than hoped for.

## Details

### South African cases (highest priority)

**Zithulele Independent School, Eastern Cape (founded 2016).** This is the closest match to our situation in the entire evidence base. It was founded by a group of parents and community members in a remote village in the former Transkei, an area with roughly 77% unemployment. It deliberately serves a mixed population: about 60% of learners come from the local rural community and the balance from families working at nearby NGOs and the Zithulele hospital, producing a mix of language, race and income under one roof (Zithulele Independent School, 2024; Ubuntu Global Connections, 2023). Governance and legal form: registered as a Non-Profit Company with CIPC, as an NPO with the Department of Social Development, and with the Eastern Cape Department of Education, and in process of PBO registration with SARS. Funding in the first five years: sliding-scale fees based on household income (every family pays something, but 74% of learners receive a fee subsidy) plus sustained bridging support from a US non-profit, Ubuntu Global Connections, which has supported it since founding in 2016. Enrolment is small, around 79 to 110 learners across grades RR to 7, with an 8:1 learner-teacher ratio. Curriculum: bilingual (each class has an English-speaking and an isiXhosa-speaking teacher), place-based and experiential. Relationship to the state system: complementary, and it reports learners outperforming peers at neighbouring schools. Community-benefit mandate: explicit, describing itself as "a school of hope, rooted in our community." Caveat: much of the detail is self-reported by the school and its US funder, and it is still a primary school, so its route into secondary grades is not yet proven.

**LEAP Science and Maths Schools (first school 2004, Langa, Cape Town).** LEAP is a network of no-fee independent high schools in township communities (Langa, Gugulethu, Crossroads, Alexandra, Diepsloot, Ga-Rankuwa and Jane Furse in rural Limpopo). It was founded by John Gilmour, a former Cape Town schoolteacher and principal, rather than by the community itself, so it is a founder-led rather than community-instigated model (LEAP/Ashoka, 2018). It is highly relevant for three reasons: its funding model (small parent contributions plus corporate social investment plus limited state subsidy plus foundations and trusts), its "three-way collaboration" that pairs each LEAP school with a more privileged partner school and neighbouring township schools, and its documented community-benefit programmes (after-hours maths tutoring, teacher training, unemployed-parent support). On its own reported figures, in 2016 LEAP achieved a 97% grade 12 pass rate and 67% bachelor-level passes, with over 90% of learners gaining admission to tertiary study; founder John Gilmour has said a study of the first five years of LEAP high school entrants showed 74% went on to tertiary education (ITWeb; LEAP). It has survived more than 20 years, which demonstrates the durability of the low-fee independent high school model in poor South African communities.

**Hermanus Waldorf School and Imhoff Waldorf School, Western Cape (mid-1990s onward).** These are the most directly relevant Western Cape examples of parent-founded schools bridging a social divide. The Hermanus Waldorf School sits geographically between Zwelihle, Mount Pleasant and Sandbaai, positioning itself as a meeting point for three cultural groups in the town (Freunde der Erziehungskunst; Hermanus Waldorf, 2025). Imhoff Waldorf School near Kommetjie was started in 1996 by a group of parents committed to alternative, environment-rooted education, and grew its high school one grade at a time from Class 8 in 2011 with support from the Southern African Federation of Waldorf Schools (Waldorf Today, 2013). Both illustrate the grade-by-grade growth path into secondary schooling and the parent-cooperative governance typical of Waldorf schools. Caveat: Hermanus Waldorf currently advertises for high school teachers, suggesting its secondary phase is still being built out, and Waldorf fee levels, even on bursary, can exclude the poorest families unless a subsidy is designed in.

**Ithuba Community College, Katlehong, Gauteng.** A Waldorf-method community college on the edge of South Africa's second-largest township, in an area with youth unemployment above 60%. It teaches grade R to grade 7 and runs a vocational Skills College for around 190 young adults a year, currently around 268 school learners (Ithuba, 2024). It has an explicit graduate mandate: that learners "contribute to the sustainable development of their households, their community and a diverse and democratic South Africa." It partners with a neighbouring high school to carry its graduates through to matric, a useful model where a small founding school cannot yet offer all twelve grades. Its dual academic-plus-vocational structure is directly relevant to our requirement to serve both university-track and vocational learners.

**Streetlight Schools, Jeppestown, Johannesburg (primary 2016, high school 2025).** Founded by Melanie Smuts, Streetlight began as a low-fee primary school in a converted inner-city shoe factory and, in its own words, "opened Streetlight Schools: Jeppestown High School in 2025, welcoming 75 Grade 8–10 learners" (Streetlight Schools, 2025). It is instructive on two points our community faces. First, community embeddedness: it surveyed local parents on affordability before opening, sourced a third of its construction team and much of its material from within a 200m radius, and trained local residents as first-time teachers. Second, the fee-collection problem: founder Melanie Smuts reports that "a significant percentage (15-20%) of parents that, even though they are financially capable of paying school fees, do not actually pay them...a problem that affects every low-fee private school in our area," and Streetlight refused the common punitive response of banning non-paying children. Its primary fees run at about R6,000 a year against a roughly R30,000 per-learner cost, the gap covered by donors. Early challenge resolved: a property partner (Bjala) subsidised its rent in the early days, which gave it stability, underlining how important a patient landlord or donated premises is in the first years.

**Section 21 public schools with a community founding role.** If you take the public route, the best-documented recent analogues are in the Eastern Cape. Vukile Tshwete Senior Secondary School at Tshoxa village near Keiskammahoek was, in the words of its SGB chairperson Andile Maki, "founded through the sacrifices of elderly people who contributed their social grants," after years of community protest; a new R56-million building opened in March 2024 and it now serves around 760 learners (GroundUp, 2024). Baleni Senior Secondary School near Xolobeni was expanded by parents who built classrooms and a staff room themselves when the province failed to (GroundUp/Daily Maverick, 2021). These show that community founding energy can be channelled into the public system via the SGB, but also that the state's infrastructure delivery is slow and often has to be substituted by the community itself. Note: no equally well-documented post-1990 case of a Western Cape farming community founding a brand-new public high school was found, so this is a partial gap in the evidence.

**Investor-founded networks (instructive but not community models).** SPARK Schools was co-founded in 2012 by Stacey Brewer and Ryan Harrison out of an MBA project at the Gordon Institute of Business Science, funded by venture capital (an initial R4.5 million round in 2012, then R28 million from Pearson's Affordable Learning Fund, reaching about R200 million by 2016), and runs on a for-profit model through the eAdvance group (Entrepreneur, 2019; Wikipedia, 2024). Nova Pioneer grew from Pioneer Academies, founded in South Africa by Chinezi Chijioke in 2013 and merged with a Kenyan school in 2015, backed by education entrepreneurs and investors (Stanford GSB, 2019). Neither involved genuine community instigation. Their useful lessons: SPARK's finding that positioning a school as cheap "switched people off" and that parents wanted an aspirational identity first and price second; and the fact that both found it easier to train new teachers in their model than to retrain veterans.

### Sub-Saharan Africa and Global South cases

**Kenya's Harambee secondary schools (1960s onward).** The classic large-scale example of communities building their own secondary schools through collective self-help ("harambee" means "pulling together"). By 1967 harambee schools outnumbered government-aided schools, and per the Keller/Anderson study "the 1976 annual private investment in harambee education was $45 million" (PubMed; Britannica). The Chavakali case in western Kenya became a lasting model of what local self-help could achieve, and many harambee schools, such as St Patrick's Bukhakunga, were later absorbed into the government system (from 1989 in that case). Lessons for our community: the harambee model demonstrates both the power of community fundraising and its central weakness, which was a chronic shortage of qualified teachers and uneven quality, and the risk that community-built schools produce credentials that do not compete in the labour market unless quality is assured. This is precisely why Umalusi accreditation matters.

**Escuela Nueva, Colombia (founded 1975).** A rural multigrade model founded by Vicky Colbert, Óscar Mogollón and Beryl Levinger that became national policy and now runs in around 20,000 rural schools in Colombia; Fundación Escuela Nueva has shared the model across 40 countries, with 12 adopting it through government policy reform (HundrED; Fundación Escuela Nueva). Its four interconnected components (curricular, teacher training, school-community, and administrative) and its use of active learning, flexible promotion and built-in community participation are the best-documented rural pedagogy in the Global South. Its running costs, once set up, were only 5 to 10% higher per pupil than conventional schools. Relevance: it shows how to run quality education across mixed ages and abilities with few teachers, and how deliberately drawing on community "funds of knowledge" keeps families as active participants. Caveat: it is primarily a primary-school model, and Colombia's 1990s decentralisation produced patchy implementation where regions could not manage teacher training.

### OECD rural models

**Lagan College, Northern Ireland (founded 1981).** The strongest cross-community precedent in the evidence. In its own written evidence to the UK Parliament's Northern Ireland Affairs Committee, it was founded in 1981 "by a group of parents...known as the All Children Together movement...Our school started with twenty-eight students and five members of staff, in a Scout hut, located next to the river Lagan," during the Troubles, with an armed police guard on the first day and some parents personally financing the project. Today it has around 1,480 students (2024/25) and a maintained mission of balancing intake, reporting 32% Protestant, 36% Roman Catholic and 32% other as of 2025 (Lagan College; Wikipedia). Governance and lessons: it is a grant-maintained school with an active Parents' Council; its founding insight is that cross-community mixing must be a deliberate, quota-managed admissions policy, not an aspiration. This is directly transferable to a school that must bridge a mission-settlement and an affluent village.

**Kura Kaupapa Māori, New Zealand (first kura 1985).** Māori-language immersion schools founded by parents and communities (whānau) who had come through the kōhanga reo preschool movement and wanted to preserve their language and knowledge. Founders fundraised and endured hardship to run kura without government assistance until the state recognised and funded them, five years after the first school, through the Education Act 1989 (Te Ara Encyclopedia; Wikipedia). Many kura began as primary schools and added wharekura (secondary departments) to keep learners in a Māori-medium environment to year 13, and the "kura teina / kura tuakana" mentoring system pairs a new school with an established one. Lessons: expect to run on community funding before any state support arrives, sequence primary-then-secondary, and use mentoring by an established school.

**Danish friskole (free school) tradition and Scottish rural place-based schools.** Denmark's free schools, rooted in the Grundtvig-Kold tradition since the 1850s, are non-profit institutions run by parent-and-governor boards and state-subsidised. Per FRISKOLERNE, 17% of Danish basic-school pupils (about 110,000 children) attend the roughly 550 free schools; the state grant equals 71% of the average per-pupil cost in a municipal school, and parents pay on average about US$2,050 per child per year (FRISKOLERNE; Scan Magazine cites 18% across pre-school to tenth grade). They are the deepest historical example of a durable parent-governed school movement and of a workable fee-plus-subsidy split. Scotland's small rural and island secondary schools (for example Sgoil Lionacleit in the Western Isles) use place-based education, shared e-learning (e-Sgoil), and embedding of local language and crafts to sustain viable secondary provision in tiny communities (Education Sciences, 2025). Lesson: place-based curriculum and shared/online delivery let small schools offer a full secondary subject range they could not staff alone.

### The South African regulatory pathway (WCED and Umalusi)

Two legal routes exist under the South African Schools Act 84 of 1996 (SASA). An independent school is created "at own cost" under sections 45 to 48 and must register with the WCED Head of Department before it may operate; it then applies to Umalusi for accreditation (gov.za/Umalusi, 2018). A public school on private property is created under section 14 via an agreement between the MEC and the landowner, and can be assigned Section 21 status to manage its own funds and governance through an SGB.

For the independent route, the governing instrument is the WCED "Regulations relating to the Registration of and Subsidies to Independent Schools," Provincial Gazette Extraordinary No. 6932 of 6 December 2011. Requirements, drawn from that framework, include: a registered juristic person with a constitution covering ownership, aims, governance, funding, admission, religion and language policy; secure tenure of premises (title or lease) with floor and site plans, a health certificate, and a municipal certificate confirming the site meets zoning and land-use by-laws; a curriculum complying with CAPS/the National Curriculum Statement; a list of educators with their SACE registration numbers (it is illegal under the SACE Act 31 of 2000 to employ an unregistered educator); a signed non-discrimination declaration under section 9(4) of the Constitution; a declaration that the school has applied to Umalusi for accreditation; proof of financial viability for at least 12 months; and a minimum enrolment declaration of around 20 learners. Applications reach the HoD roughly six months before the intended opening. Provisional registration is granted where compliance is incomplete, with an expiry date by which full compliance must be met, and it must be stated on the school's letterhead; it typically runs on an academic-year basis and lapses if conversion to full registration is not achieved.

The state subsidy is governed by section 48 of SASA and the National Norms and Standards for School Funding (Government Gazette 19347 of 1998, as amended). A school must be registered for a full year before it can apply (applications by 31 March of the preceding year). Subsidies are paid on a five-point sliding scale benchmarked to the Provincial Average Estimate Per Learner cost in public schools, from a maximum of 60% for the lowest-fee schools down to 15%, and schools charging more than 2.5 times the provincial per-learner cost get no subsidy. A low-fee community school would fall in the upper subsidy bands (ISASA; NNSSF).

Common first-three-year failure points, from the Western Cape Provincial Parliament record (Q&A, MEC Debbie Schäfer, 12 March 2021) and IOL/Cape Times reporting on more than 30 non-compliant WCED independent schools: educators not registered with SACE (the most cited problem, worsened by there being no SACE regional office in the Western Cape); Umalusi accreditation not applied for or unaffordable; premises relocated to sites not approved by the municipality; and financial non-viability, including unpaid municipal accounts (the Darul Islam Islamic High School had R1.2 million in municipal arrears and irregular teacher salaries) that push schools to hire unqualified teachers. Under Regulation 8(2) the WCED issues a notice of intent to withdraw registration and subsidy, with a right to make written representations.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 (months 0 to 12, before opening).**
- Constitute a non-profit company (NPC) under the Companies Act with a governing board that is representative of both the mission-settlement and the village populations, and register it as an NPO and PBO. Follow the Zithulele legal template exactly: NPC with CIPC, NPO with Social Development, registration with the provincial education department, PBO with SARS.
- Secure premises with title or a long lease and get the municipal zoning/land-use certificate in hand before you apply. Unapproved premises are one of the top three causes of WCED refusal.
- Draft a sliding-scale fee policy from the outset so that every family contributes something and the poorest receive subsidies. This is the mechanism that holds a cross-income community together and, by keeping fees low, positions you for the 60% subsidy band.
- Line up a bridging funder or patient landlord for the first three to five years. Every durable case here (Zithulele, LEAP, Streetlight, the kura, Lagan) relied on non-fee income before it was self-sustaining.

**Stage 2 (the WCED and Umalusi application).**
- Register every educator with SACE before you submit, and budget for competitive salaries. The evidence is unambiguous that thin finances leading to unqualified teachers is the single most common reason schools fail compliance.
- Apply for Umalusi accreditation in parallel and budget for its fees. Present a CAPS-compliant curriculum.
- Decide the independent-versus-Section-21 question on this basis: choose independent if you want control, speed and a distinctive mission and can raise bridging finance; choose Section 21 public if funded teacher posts matter more than autonomy and you can tolerate slow state infrastructure delivery. A hybrid path used elsewhere is to start independent and negotiate later absorption, as many Kenyan harambee schools and Māori kura did.

**Stage 3 (opening and first three years).**
- Open with the foundation secondary grades (grade 8, then add a grade a year) rather than all of grades 8 to 12 at once, following Imhoff, Streetlight and the kura. This controls cost and lets systems mature.
- Design cross-community mixing explicitly: a managed admissions balance in the Lagan mould, a bilingual staffing model in the Zithulele mould (an Afrikaans and an English or isiXhosa teacher per class as appropriate to our valley), and shared events that force contact.
- Offer both an academic and a vocational or skills stream, using the Ithuba dual model and, where you cannot staff a subject, Scotland's shared/online e-learning approach and partnership with a neighbouring high school.
- Write a community-benefit mandate into the founding documents: adult skills, tutoring, use of facilities by the wider valley. This is a common thread in every school here that survived and it also strengthens later subsidy and donor applications.

**Benchmarks that would change the plan.** If you cannot secure three to five years of bridging finance, do not open an independent school; pursue the Section 21 public route instead. If fee-paying uptake in a pre-opening parent survey (do one, as Streetlight did) cannot cover even 40% of running costs at sliding-scale levels, the enrolment base is too thin for a standalone high school and you should consider a combined school or a satellite of an existing school. If SACE-registered teachers cannot be recruited to a small town at our salary offer, prioritise a teacher-training and mentoring partnership (the LEAP and kura-teina models) before expanding grades.

## Caveats
- Several of the most relevant cases (Zithulele, LEAP, Ithuba, Streetlight) rely on self-reported enrolment, pass rates and fee-subsidy figures from the schools or their funders. Treat performance claims as indicative, not audited.
- The evidence for a directly comparable Western Cape farming-community-founded new public high school since 1990 is thin; the closest documented community-founding cases are Eastern Cape public schools, and the Western Cape parent-founded examples are independent Waldorf schools that skew towards middle-income families.
- The exact rand value of the provincial per-learner subsidy benchmark and the current fee thresholds should be confirmed against the latest WCED annual subsidy determination and the exact text of Provincial Gazette 6932 of 2011 before you finalise a budget, as the subsidy percentages here derive from ISASA and the national norms rather than the current-year WCED figure.
- Harambee and Escuela Nueva are included for their founding and pedagogical lessons; both are historically older or primary-focused and should not be read as turnkey secondary-school templates.
